# Pro play — high-level concepts from the competitive scene

Sourced from competitive guides, pro-scene consensus writeups, and expert-tips compilations.
Confidence here works differently from `numbers.md`: `[V]` means **a credible source asserts it**,
not that it is a measurable fact. Tactical doctrine is opinion with evidence. Where sources
disagree, see `perspectives.md`.

Analysis marked `[I]` connects this doctrine to the verified numbers — that linkage is the part
worth the most, because most guides give the rule without the arithmetic behind it.

---

## 1. The structural mistake that defines mid-ranks

> "Winning small fights across the map while losing cashout fights" — named as **the most common
> structural mistake in Platinum.** `[V]`

`[I]` This is the single most important line in this file, and the verified numbers explain exactly
why it's true. An elimination pays $500. A completed cashout pays the station pot — $8,000 on an
*early* box, more later. **You have to win sixteen duels to match one early cashout.** Players who
feel like they're playing well while losing are usually farming the $500 stream and conceding the
$8,000 one.

The correction isn't "stop fighting" — combat is real revenue (`macro.md` §2). It's that fights
must be *near the money*.

## 2. Before the box: rotation

**Don't sprint to every vault.** Read the arena first. `[V]` Three questions before committing `[V]`:

1. **Where are the other teams?**
2. **Is this station defendable?**
3. **Can my team recover if the first fight fails?**

> "A Cashout started in a room you cannot hold is usually a gift to the third team." `[V]`

**Route discipline `[V]`:**
- Identify the most defensible station **before** picking up the box — not after.
- Use roofs, stairs, and buildings to cover the carrier's path.
- Keep one player **slightly behind the carrier** to punish chasers.
- Deposit only when **two players can immediately hold angles** around the station.
- After depositing, **spread three sightlines.** Do not stack on one wall.
- Group tight **only when a steal is imminent** — to trade kills without dying together.

`[I]` "Deposit only when two players can hold angles" is a stricter rule than this substrate
originally derived, and it's better. It converts the vague "don't deposit solo" into a checkable
condition.

## 3. Defending: three layers, three roles

**The three layers `[V]`:** early spotting → entry denial → steal prevention.

> The classic failure is all three players "staring directly at the cashbox" — which loses the first
> two layers entirely. `[V]`

**The roles `[V]`:**

| Role | Position | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Opener** | Outer angle or upper floor | Spots early, calls pushes, breaks the first entry |
| **Support** | Within revive range of both | Heals, resets, covers trades |
| **Anchor** | *Near* the station, not glued to it | Denies steals, survives the final window |

> "The anchor needs to stay alive more than they need to look impressive." `[V]`
> Hold short angles with escape routes. `[V]`

`[I]` The anchor role is the human implementation of the arithmetic in `macro.md` §4: the job is
denying any 7 consecutive uncontested seconds. The anchor is the player whose entire purpose is
that the clock never runs clean. Their K/D is irrelevant.

`[I]` Note the Support role is positioned *by revive range* — that is coin economy expressed as
positioning. A support out of revive range is costing the team coins whether or not they're winning
their duel.

## 4. Stealing: the thief goes last

**The sequence `[V]`:**
1. One player draws attention with **damage or destruction**.
2. A second **blocks line of sight**.
3. The third **starts the steal**.

> "The thief should be the *last* part, not the first." `[V]`
> Do not start the interaction until defenders are forced to look away or reposition. `[V]`

**The panic error `[V]`:**
> "The biggest Ranked Cashout mistake is starting a steal because the timer makes everyone panic."

If the stealing player takes damage, they **instantly call stick or drop**. `[V]`

**Setting `[V]`:** there is a toggle-interact option (rather than hold). Test it in casual first —
accidental cancels are worse than the ergonomics gain.

`[I]` The steal is 7 seconds `[V, numbers.md]`. A distraction that buys 4 seconds is not enough.
This is why the sequencing matters: you are not creating a *window*, you are creating a window that
outlasts a fixed, known duration. Plan distractions in units of "does this last 7 seconds?"

## 5. Objective manipulation — the part most players never learn

These are the highest-leverage `[V]` items in this file because they change the geometry of a fight
rather than winning it.

- **Drop the station to you.** Destroy the floor beneath a Cashout Station with C4 or explosives and
  it falls to your level. Defenders usually follow, into a fight you chose. `[V]`
- **Interrupt a steal by destroying the ground under it.** If someone is stealing, blowing up the
  floor they're standing on cancels the interaction. `[V]` `[I]` **This is the single best
  counter-steal tool available** — it does not require winning a duel, and against a 7-second
  interaction it only needs to land once.
- **Relocate the station with a jump pad.** Place a jump pad beneath the station to launch it to a
  more advantageous position. `[V]`
- **Collapse the building.** Destroy more than half the ground-floor walls (Sledgehammer is fastest)
  and the structure comes down on an entrenched team. `[V]`
- **Pyro grenades on ceilings** ignite enemies on the floor above — specifically effective against
  cashout positions. `[V]`
- **Extend explosive range** by attaching C4 or mines to throwable objects (chairs, pots), then
  throwing them. `[V]`
- **Deny access points.** Destroy ziplines and ladders to slow pursuers and buy escape time. `[V]`
- **Canisters** — explosive (red), smoke (grey), gas (green), goo (pink) — are free gadgets lying
  around the map. Heavy throws them furthest. `[V]`

**Goo `[V]`:** builds walls for cover in blown-open rooms, seals flanking routes, blocks access to
the cashout terminal, bridges to unreachable buildings, and stacks under yourself (Heavy) to gain
height.

`[I]` Goo blocking terminal access deserves emphasis: it attacks the 7-second interaction directly.
An enemy who must first clear goo is an enemy whose steal window just got longer than 7 seconds.

## 6. Movement

Ordered roughly by value-per-effort. `[V]` throughout.

- **Sprint queuing** — press sprint while airborne holding forward; the animation queues so you land
  already sprinting. Cheapest real gain in the game.
- **Slide onto jump pads** rather than sprinting into them — momentum carries.
- **Window ledge climb** — hold jump while exiting a window facing inward to grab the ledge above,
  skipping stairs entirely.
- **Horizontal jump pad placement** — launch sideways rather than up for aggressive entries.
- **Stair climb cancels** for winding staircases; **climb boosts** give a small speed burst.
- **Bunny hopping** to chain jumps and hold speed.
- **Superglide** — described as the game's most advanced movement tech. Jump and mantle, W+D while
  looking at goo beside you, then turn the camera back where you want to go while holding A.

**Most of this degrades badly on Heavy.** `[V]` Heavy can still reach rooftops, but movement tech is
not where Heavy's value lies.

`[I]` For a Light player this is not optional polish. Light is priced at 150 HP `[V]` on the
assumption you convert mobility into avoided damage. A Light who doesn't move well is simply a worse
Medium.

## 7. Third-party discipline

- **If you don't know where the third squad is, assume they are about to third-party you.** `[V]`
- **Don't fight far from stations after a successful deposit.** Chasing weak enemies wastes time and
  exposes the objective. `[V]`
- **Score-aware fight selection `[V]`:** if you're ahead, refuse distant fights. If you're behind,
  track the team that can overtake you specifically.

`[I]` A qualifying match has four teams `[V]`. When you deposit, you alert all of them `[V]`.
"Assume a third party" is not caution — it is the base case.

## 8. Named mistakes that lose ranked games `[V]`

1. **Late wipes.** Three dead players near the end of a cashout is a free steal window. **Keep one
   player alive**, outside or on a nearby floor.
   `[I]` This independently validates the substrate's founding conclusion (`situations.md` §2): the
   last player alive should preserve the body, not spend it. It also sharpens it — the survivor
   should be *deliberately held back*, not merely the accidental survivor.
2. **Post-deposit ego fights.** Chasing after a successful deposit hands the objective to a third team.
3. **Automatic revives.** Reviving inside an enemy sightline gives them two eliminations instead of
   one. Move, block vision, or reposition first.
   `[I]` At $500 per elimination `[V]`, a careless revive is a $1,000 gift *plus* two coins.

## 9. Habit and discipline `[V]`

- **Master one build.** Constantly switching between Light, Medium, and Heavy is named as the biggest
  thing holding low-ranked players back. The fastest climbers know one build deeply.
- **Game sense beats mechanics at the top.** Top players are not mechanically flawless; they make
  better decisions more consistently.
- **Two seconds of listening prevents half your unnecessary deaths.**
- **Aim upper chest, not head** — higher consistency than hunting headshots.
- **Crouch while reloading** to shrink your hitbox.
- **Let the Medium defib** rather than attempting manual revives.
  `[I]` Both are free of coin cost `[V]`; the defib is simply faster and survivable under fire.
- **Commit to one sensitivity for at least 20 hours** before changing it.
- **Two losses in a row → take a 20-minute break.** Forcing a third almost always loses too.
- **Consistency beats highlights.** Ranked uses four placement rounds, then league tiers Bronze
  through Diamond, with Ruby for the top 500. A team that qualifies often climbs better than one
  that looks brilliant occasionally.
