# THE BOOK OF HUNNY

---

## A NOTE BEFORE BEGINNING
*(Nota de la Autora — voz de Sallie, fijada)*

These stories are not mine — or at least, the ones from before I arrived are not. Those belong to a bear, and the bear could not write them down — writing was never a thing for bears; it was a thing for owls, who were good at it, and for me, who was taught. So he told them, and I wrote them; and if anything in those pages is wrong, it is my hand that is wrong and not his remembering, for his remembering is the surest thing I know.

Him I shall call Pooh, for that is the name I gave him when first I found him, when he could not tell me his own and neither could anyone else; and the name he bore before that, in the days these stories tell of, he kept it for himself, and the only ones who could tell us are not here.

He was very small when these things happened, and he asks me to say so at once, so that you will forgive him the parts where he was asleep, or eating, or both — which, he wishes me to add, are not the same thing, though in his experience they can be arranged quite close together.

He also asks that the honey in this book be spelled his way. I have looked into the matter carefully, and I find I have no argument against it that is better than his argument for it, which is that *hunny* is how it tastes.

The later stories are mine, from when I came to know the Wood myself, and you must not expect too much of them. They are smaller stories, I think, because I was there for them; the great things always seem to have happened just before one arrives.

I should warn you before you begin: nothing alarming happens in this book. Nobody is lost for longer than an afternoon, and nobody is hungry for longer than it takes to do something about it. If it is alarms you want, you must look in other books. This one is about a Queen, and everything that follows; because it all started there.

— S. S.

# Chapter One
### *With Hunny begins*

It all started with hunny — that much I know. But how it all began, only Mica knew, for only Mica could hear the whispers of Mellea, since she was the smallest of all and the only one able to hear a thing so tiny as Mellifera Regina Favi Creatrice Prima, the Bee Queen of Queens, the maker of the first drop of hunny. So Mellea taught Mica. And Mica told Pooh, and Pooh told me, so that by the time it reached this page it had been passed along like a made-up bedtime story from one to the next, and everyone told it a little differently — each teller adding a little and forgetting a little — until nobody is quite sure what was in it first, but everybody is sure it was sweet. So it may not be the right story. If it has come out a touch sticky, I do not think it is because everything began with hunny — though it did — but because it was told by so many, and written down by me so late: for I first heard it as a small girl, and did not write it until the fifth telling, because that was when I had learnt to write.

What Mica heard was this: that before all there was hunny, because there is no sweeter way to begin; and before the hunny there was Mellea; and before Mellea it is better not to ask, for not even Mica dared.

From her sweetness came everything that is sweet, which is to say very nearly everything. The flowers came, because the bees must have somewhere to go of a morning. The streams came, because sweetness likes to travel. The warm came, and then the cold after it, so that there might be a difference between them. The meadows came, and the long grass, and the small good places where small good creatures might one day decide to live.

Maybe the trees did not come from her. Whether they stood there already, or came afterwards, I do not know — whether Mica never knew, or simply forgot. But this much is certain: that Mellea had need of them, for her hunnycombs, and for everything sweet that comes with them.

Now Mellea looked at what she had begun, and saw that it was sweet, and saw also that sweet was not enough. For a world that is only sweet is rather like a song that is only one note: very nice for a moment, and then you start wishing something would happen. She saw that the Wood, to be a Wood, would need more than hunny — and so she imagined it; and imagining was all she needed to do, being who she was, the Queen of Queens. She imagined the ones who could help her make the One Hundred Acre Wood into all that it wanted to be. And so the mothering nymphs blossomed into the Wood, because that was what the Queen desired; and what the Queen desires, blossoms.

First she saw that the Wood would need listening-to, which is the rarest kind of care there is, and the first that everybody forgets; and besides, her own whisper would want an ear, for there is no use in a whisper that nobody hears. So the first mother she made was the smallest — and to this one she gave no grand name at all. So she was only ever Mica. She was the littlest mouse in all the Wood, and she could hear the littlest sounds, because she made so few herself; she could hear a leaf decide to fall before it fell, and she could hear Mellea's whisper, which no one else could hear at all. And that is why this story even exists: because the first thing the Wood ever made was someone to listen, and Mica listened to everything, and forgot none of it. Mica thought that being small was the best of all things to be, and would tell you so, if you were quiet enough to hear it.

Then she saw the Wood would need feeding. Not the hunny kind — that was hers already — but the everyday kind, the kind that keeps you going. And she knew a thing about feeding that not everyone knows: that creatures who eat alone stay strangers, and creatures who get together to share a meal at the same place become more like family. So she made a mother for that, and said: *I give you the name Mugiente; but a mother needs a loving name as well as a grand one, so to all the Wood you shall be Mu.* And Mu cared to feed the whole Wood. Her milk was sweet, in its way — but it was not hunny, and was never meant to be; and that was the first thing the Wood learnt without being told: that not everything sweet is hunny, and not everything good is a treat.

Then she saw the Wood would need somewhere deep — somewhere it could go on learning more the deeper it went, the way the best questions only get bigger the longer you look at them. So she made a mother for that, and said: *I give you the name Cantilena; but to the Wood you shall be Lena.* And to Lena, being a grand whale, she gave the water, all of it, to be hers and hers alone — the lake, and the streams, and whatever lived in them that no one else would ever meet. Lena was the biggest of them all, and the wisest, and you would think someone so big and so wise would be very busy and very serious. But Lena spent most of her time playing — always making up new games of her own, with no rules and no winner at the end of them. When the others asked why someone so deep would do something so pointless, she said that was exactly why, and went on doing it, and never explained any better than that, because she did not think it needed explaining.

And last she saw the Wood would need thinking, and understanding — for a thing nobody bothers to understand is only half a thing — and she knew that understanding does not belong to the loudest, nor to the one who is sure, but to the one who will say *I do not know yet* and then go and find out. So she made a mother duck for that, and said: *I give you the name Undina; but to the Wood you shall be Unda.* Now Unda was the only one who could go between the forest and the lake alike like most ducks nowadays, at home in both and bound to neither, and she would roam from the one to the other looking at everything twice, because she said you could not trust a thing you had only looked at once.

The four mothers raised the Wood between them. And before very long they would raise something else between them too — but that is the next part, and it begins, as the best parts do, with someone very small, and very hungry, and very much wanted, though he did not yet know it.

In those days the Wood was very new, and so was everyone in it, and nobody had been told yet how anything was meant to go. This made the first of everything a great surprise. The first rain came down one afternoon with no warning at all, and the mothers — each in her own part of the Wood, for they did not yet know one another — stood in it wondering whether to be alarmed, until they found it was only water from above instead of below, and rather pleasant, and that it made the flowers louder. The first cold came later, and was less pleasant; and whoever had arranged for the cold to come after the warm, one could not help feeling they might have arranged it the other way about. But then the warm came back, brighter for having been gone — and that, it turned out, was the whole point of it: for a thing you have all the time you do not notice at all, and a thing that goes and returns you are glad of twice, once for the having and once for the having-again. And the warm did return; and then went; and then returned. It was a long while before anyone was sure it meant to keep doing that — that the world went round and round, and not only forward and away — but it did, and learning so is one of the first comforts there is, and the Wood had it early.

All this time, and below all of it, Mellea was at her own quiet work, which was the work of hunny. She made no great noise about it. The bees went out in the mornings — that was what the flowers had been for, all along — and came back heavy, and the hunnycombs filled, slow and golden and patient, in the warm dark inside the trees the trees had lent her. And the Wood grew sweeter without anyone deciding that it should; for that is the way of Mellea's sweetness, coming off her the way warmth comes off a fire, without the fire meaning anything by it. So there was always, under everything else, a great and growing store of sweetness, filling and filling. Nobody thought much about it. That is rather the trouble with sweetness: it is so busy being lovely that one forgets to wonder what it is for.

For how the mothers met it was Unda who came to know them all first, because Unda was the only one looking. The others were content to be where they were and to do what they did; but Unda could not let a thing alone until she had poked it, climbed it, turned it over, or fallen off it. She had tried to stand on the very top of the tallest rock, to see whether the sky was any nearer from up there ,it was not; and she had tried to drink the whole lake, to find out where it ended, she did not find out; and she had spent one whole afternoon trying to walk into the forest and the lake at the very same time, with a foot in each, and so that's how she came to be so often wet on one side only.

She came to know Mu first — or rather, she found the great crowd of small creatures all going the same way, and followed them to see what was so interesting, and the interesting thing was Mu.

"Why does everyone follow you about?" said Unda.

"Because I feed them," said Mu.

"Feed them what?"

"Milk, mostly."

"And what is milk?"

"Something one has," said Mu.

"Do I have it?"

"Oh! You have it as well?" agreed Mu, who had taken this for a statement rather than a question, it being the sort of thing she was used to agreeing with.

"No, I don't have it *too*," said Unda, trying to clear the matter up.

"Then I wonder who has the third," said Mu.

"Third what?"

"Of milk, of course."

"So there is *more* milk?"

"Well, if there are two, there must be three, I should think," said Mu simply.

And so, being curious as she was, and not in the least nearer to knowing what milk was, Unda offered to help Mu look for the other two or three milks — though it must be said that Unda was a good deal more troubled about the mislaid milk than Mu was, the milk having got lost only in their heads, which is the hardest place there is to look.

Lena she came to know after, and with Lena she stayed longest and came back oftenest; for Lena was the deepest of them, and kept the things that are held at the bottom of the water and the bottom of most other matters too, and Unda wanted all of it. They would talk the whole of an afternoon away, Lena saying deep slow things and Unda asking what was under them, and then what was under that. It was Lena who told her that the water remembered being rain, and the rain remembered being the lake, so that nothing was ever quite lost, only moved along and waiting to come round again; and Unda asked whether that was true of everything or only of water, and Lena said she rather thought everything, but that water was the one place you could watch it happen. I have set that down the way it was told to me, though I am not sure I have it right, although I don't really know what they meant.

And Mica — well, nobody came to know Mica. Mica was simply discovered to have been there the whole time, listening, the way the smallest one in any house is always there the whole time.

As for the flying, that came in among all this, and happened by accident. Unda had been up a tall rock looking at something twice, as usual, and she leaned a little too far out in the looking, and down she went — except that she did not go down. She went, to her very great surprise, *up*; and her first clear thought, with the whole Wood tilting away beneath her, was that she must be falling after all, only upside down, and that the ground had got itself above her by some arrangement nobody had troubled to mention. It took her a good while to work out that this was not falling at all, but a new thing, and that the new thing was hers. Flying had until then belonged to Mellea alone, and to the bees, who never explained how; and that's how Unda learnt to fall upside down.

Now it was Mu who made them into something more than four motherly nymphs who happened to know one another. For Mu feed them all most nights, so they got used to the same place to share a meal — Lena bringing what the water gave, and Unda what she had found in her roaming, and Mica sitting small among them hearing everything, and Mu seeing to it that all fell full and nobody ate alone. And little by little, thanks to Mu, without any of them quite choosing it, the four became the family they meant to be all along, which was Mellea had Mu for; for that is how families are made, not by deciding to be one, but by sitting down to enough suppers together that you cannot remember when you were not.

It was at one of those suppers, on an ordinary night with the others gathered round, that Unda told the only one thing they all forgot. In her flying, she said, she had passed that day over the deepest part of the Wood and seen, far off, what she took for a tree. There is nothing strange in a tree; the Wood is made of them. What was strange was that this one was roaming. She had come down out of the air with caution — with the carefulness you keep for a thing you have no name for — and when she came near she saw it was not a tree at all, and not an animal at same time. "Salute," she ventured to say. And "Salute" had come back, said slowly, the way someone speaks who has all the time there is and no wish to spend it quickly. She wanted to ask what are you? But decided in who are you? So on that slow deep voice she said here name was Äldandil and some more, for that was her name or the start of it, for she said her name was still growing and grew a little more each year; and because in those days nobody yet counted the years, her name was still short enough to be spelled in one sentence. She seemed like a mother of the trees, her concerned laid in the raising of them. But nothing more was ever known of the roaming tree mother than this one telling of Unda's — for she was never seen again, nor sung of, nor spoken, in all the days of the Wood. And only Pooh remember that Unda told him this once, it was before he slept, so it counted as a bedtime story, but Pooh thought it was a memory, and so do I.

So the Wood had everything. It had feeding, and friendship, and seasons that came round again, and somewhere deep, and somebody to listen, but no Pooh bear in sight; and the strange part — the Wood was there thanks to hunny with so much sweetness and nothing to do with it. All that hunny, going round to nobody; a whole golden gladness with no one to be glad of it. Mica felt it first, being the one who feels the smallest things: a sort of quiet in the middle of all the plenty, a hush shaped like somebody who was not there yet. She could not say what it was, and nor could any of them.

And then, one day, it knew.

# Chapter Two
### *In Which we meet the cub*

The hunny was sweet, and there was ever so much of it, and that much sweetness is only half of a thing. It is like a present, all wrapped up and waiting: a present nobody opens is only a box with a bow, however lovely; it does not become a present until you have someone to give it to. So all that sweetness was waiting to be a real gift and not only a pretty one — and what it was waiting for was a hungry heart. A sweetness somebody wants, with all of them, is a joy. And the whole of the difference is one hungry heart.

And one day Mellea found the heart. She came upon him among the hunnycombs — a small brown bear, very round, eating her hunny as fast as ever a thing was eaten, with both paws and no manners and the whole of himself, the way you eat something when you have wanted it your whole life and have only just found out that it exists. He did not stop when she came. He did not know enough yet to stop, or to be sorry, or to be anything at all but glad; and the gladness of him, sticky to the ears and not the least bit ashamed, was the tenderest thing the Queen had ever seen. So much sweetness she had made, and given away, and watched lie sweet — and here at last was someone who wanted it, wanted it like that, with his whole small self. The gift had found its hungry heart.

What was he called, in those first days? That I truly do not know, and I will not pretend to. For me he was always my bear; and I called him Pooh the Sanders, because he was mine, and my name was Sallie Sanders, and it seemed to me that a bear of one's own ought to share one's own name so it doesn't get lost. And that, when you are a girl, is very nearly all a bear needs to be. Mine.

A bear, it turns out, is a great deal of work, and four mothers is about the right number for one, which may be why he was given four. Now of all the things a cub may do with a day, Pooh had decided early that two were worth more than the rest put together: the first was eating hunny, which needs no explaining, and the second — a good way behind the first, but a long way ahead of everything else — was playing with Lena. The rest of his education had to fit itself in around those two, and mostly did, in the way that important things will always make room for each other if you let them; and letting them is very nearly the whole of a bear's disposition.

Mu fed him, which was the first thing and the most often; and the very first he learned, came at her side, when he learned that the milk was not hunny. He had expected it to be. He had expected everything to be hunny, in those days, hunny being the only thing he yet knew the taste of and a very good thing to judge all others by. So when Mu fed him and it was warm and good and *not sweet*, he was, for a moment, disappointed — the way you are disappointed when you go to sip your cocoa and pick up your mother's teacup by mistake — not bad, only not at all the warm sweet thing you had your heart set on. But the milk kept him going in a way the hunny never quite did; the hunny made him glad, and the milk made him *able to be glad tomorrow*, and little by little he came to understand, without anyone explaining, that these were two different jobs, and that a bear needs both, and that it would be a poor sort of world where everything good was a treat. "Not everything sweet is hunny," he said once, very pleased with himself, having worked it out; and Mu said yes, that was about the size of it.

Mica taught him to listen, which she did mostly by being so quiet that you had to. He learned that there were small sounds under the big ones, and smaller ones under those, and that the smallest sounds were often the ones most worth the catching — a thing he forgot, later, the way everyone forgets it, but which served him well while he remembered. And he learned from Mica that to be grateful for a thing you must first *notice* it, and that most ingratitude in the world is not unkindness at all but only not noticing, which is a gentler fault and an easier one to mend.

Playing with Lena was the second business of his cubhood, and he gave it very nearly the seriousness he gave the first — which is to say, none at all, and all of it, both at once, the way a cub is serious about the things that matter most.

Lena had a different game every day, and sometimes more than one at once. There was, first and best, the throwing of things into the water simply to watch them go in. Stones were good, and sticks were good, and a particularly fat acorn was very good indeed, and it did not matter in the least what happened after; the whole of the pleasure was in the throwing and the splash. Then Lena had a game where Pooh had to climb trees: some were firm and easy to climb, others bent as you tried to go up, and others snapped when you tried to go too high, making a bear drop, and laugh when it happened. As Pooh got better at throwing things Lena taught him to skip stones, and at that he was really good — when I knew him I saw him send one out a hundred skips, which I should not have believed had I not been standing right there.

Then there was the game of guessing where Lena would come up. She would go down at one end of the lake, slow and enormous, and Pooh would fix his eyes on the spot and wait, perfectly certain, and she would come up somewhere else entirely — behind him, as often as not, with a great gentle whoosh that knocked him over with surprise every single time. He never once guessed right, and Lena, who could have come up wherever she pleased, took a quiet care to come up wherever he was not, because being wrong and toppled over and laughing is, to a cub, very much better than being right.

Then there was the making of things in the sand of the shore. Pooh learnt to shape trees with the sand, which came out like lumps, and animals, which came out like smaller lumps; he made Mu, and a duck that he said was Unda though it was difficult to be sure, and a mouse so small it was mostly just a poke of his claw, which seemed about right for Mica. He built the whole Wood by the water's edge, in lumps, and was prouder of it than of anything, and the lake came up in the night and took it gently away, so that there was fresh sand to build it again the next day, which suited everyone.

And then, when they were tired, there were the talking games — the riddles, and the puzzles, and the jokes that made him laugh so hard he had to sit down, though he was already sitting. Lena knew a great many, and she was clever about which she gave him. At the start, when he was very small, they were the simple sort — *what is brown?* — and Pooh would shout out everything brown he could think of, the earth and a stick and a bit of old bark and, after a while, himself; and the whole fun of it was that there was no end to the brown things, so that you could go on being right forever and never run out. Then, as the days went by and Pooh got better at the game, Lena gave him cleverer ones — *what is the loudest thing that makes no sound?* — and these he could turn over for half an afternoon, getting nowhere lovely, the two of them tangled up in the guessing until they were laughing too hard to go on. The later ones, he told me, my bear never solved a single one. That was not what they were for. They were for the tangle, and the laughing, and the going-nowhere-together, which Pooh thought was the cleverest thing anyone had ever invented, and he was very probably right.

And Unda — Unda did not so much teach him as drag him along behind her while she found things out, which came to the same thing in the end. From Unda he learned that *I don't know* is not a sad thing to say but an exciting one, because it is the noise the world makes just before it gets bigger. She was forever wrong about something and forever delighted to discover it, and Pooh, trotting after her, learned that being wrong is only being right a little earlier than you wanted to be, and nothing at all to mind.

So he was fed, and he was taught to listen, and to play, and to wonder; and being a bear, he took all four lessons in the order he found most natural, which is to say all at once, and muddled, and mostly while eating. This is how bears learn. It is not tidy, but it holds.

It was Unda who noticed, Unda being the one who noticed things, having had so much practice at being wrong that she had learnt to look twice at everything, in case it had gone and changed while she wasn't watching. She had been wrong about a great many matters that season — about whether the far side of the pond was farther than the near side, which it was not, being exactly as far, a thing she took rather personally — so it was Unda, as it happened, who was watching the afternoon Pooh went after the hunny.

Now the bees kept their hunny high in the old tree, and they kept it with a great deal of feeling, so that a bear who climbed straight up after it came straight down again wearing most of the hive on his nose. Pooh had done that once, and once was enough. This time he did something else. There was a young tree growing beside the old one, the bendy whippy sort, far too green to be any use for climbing, which is exactly why no one ever climbed it. Pooh climbed it. And the higher he went the more it bowed under him, the way a green tree will, until the top of it leaned right over and set him down soft against the back of the hive-tree — round the back, where the bees were not looking; for the bees were all watching the front of the hive for bears, there being, in their experience, no bear daft enough to come at them by the wrong tree, from the wrong direction.

"How ever did you know to do it that way?" said Unda.

"Do what?"

"Get the hunny. That way."

"Oh — you only lick it, and swallow."

"Not the eating part. The getting-it part. Round the back, by bending the whole tree over. I should never in my life have thought of it."

Pooh licked his paw, which was the part that mattered, and gave it some thought. "Dunno," he said. "The bending game, I expect. The one Lena and I play, where you have to find out which trees will bend, and how far, under a bear. That is the tricky part, the how-far; for when you get it wrong you come down hard — sometimes in a funny way, and sometimes in the way that hurts."

And Unda went quiet, which was not like her.

For Unda was the one who liked to know what things were for; and she saw, all at once, what the playing had been for. All those afternoons at nothing in particular — the stones thrown in for the splash, the riddles nobody ever solved, the going nowhere for whole days together — and out of all that nothing had come a bear who could get hunny past a whole tree of bees without a single sting. Lena had never once taught him anything. She had only played with him, day after day, until the playing had done it on its own. And that, Unda thought, was the cleverest thing she had ever seen anybody not do.

So she stood in the grass and said nothing, and was, just that once, right and quiet both together; and of the two, it was the quiet that would have surprised everybody more.

Pooh had already gone back to the hunny. It seemed to him the only sensible thing to do with it.

# Chapter Three
### *The Days of Bob*

It was about this time, when Pooh was still small but not so small as he had been, that he met Bob; though "met" is not quite the word, and what really happened is a thing Pooh was sorry about for a long while.

He was walking — not going anywhere, just walking, which is the finest sort of walking there is — and thinking about hunny, as he generally was, when he felt something wanting to go *crunch* under his paw. Now a great many things go crunch in a wood, and most of them are leaves, and a leaf does not mind being stepped on, having no particular plans. But this nearly crunch was different. It sounded like a *"Bob!"* — quite loudly.

Pooh lifted his paw, which is the first thing you do. And there, in the print of it, was a beetle: very small, very cross, and not nearly so flat as he might have been — for it is a lucky beetle that is trodden on by the fluffiest bear in the Wood, whose paws were mostly pillow, and who had only managed to *fold* him, which is undignified, but the sort of thing that comes right again. Pooh did not know that yet. For one terrible moment he thought he had done the worst thing there is, which is to end somebody, by accident, while thinking about hunny.

"Pardono!" said Pooh. "I am so very sorry. I did not see you, because you are — " and here he stopped, because *small* seemed a rude thing to say to someone you had just stepped on, even if it was the reason.

"Small," said the beetle, getting his legs back in their proper order one at a time. "Yes. I know. Everyone notices sooner or later. You noticed it a tad harder than most." And he said it without much heat, the way you say a thing that is simply true and cannot be helped, which made Pooh feel worse than any amount of shouting would have done.

Now Pooh did not know, then, the thing he came to know later: that being big is mostly a way of being able to hurt small things without meaning to, and that the whole job of being big, the only honest one, is to mind it. But he felt the beginning of it, that morning, standing over the folded beetle with his heart gone all wrong inside him; and feeling the beginning of a thing is how you start to learn it.

So he did the only thing he could think of, which was to make it right, as much as a thing like that can be made right. He sat with the beetle the rest of the morning. He helped him unfold, which mostly meant staying very still and feeling sorry sometimes for not really helping, since there was not a great deal a bear's enormous paws could do for so small a folding. And when the beetle was more or less himself again, Pooh said — I thought you *Bob*.

Oh, that's my name?

Oh ok, nice to meet you Bob.

And that's how he got to be named Bob, for no one has ever bothered to name him anything. Even though you got named for the noise you made by nearly being squashed by a fluffy bear, if you think about it, it isn't the most dignified way to come by a name — But the beetle tried it on, the way you try on a name, and found it fit; and *Bob* he was, from that morning, the first of all the Roberts there would ever be in that Wood, though neither of them knew it, and only one of them would ever find out.

The next thing Pooh learnt, he learnt from Bob, and it was the size of himself; which is a great deal to learn from somebody so small.

For Pooh was a lot of bear, and Bob was hardly any beetle, and that is the sort of difference you have to mind. A bear who does not mind it can fold a beetle without even meaning to — Pooh had very nearly done it once, and once was plenty. So now, wherever Bob went, Pooh carried himself the way you carry a full cup across a room: slowly, and all of a piece. He hadn't a word for it. He only found, in his paws, that there is a right amount of bear to give a thing — and that when the thing is a Bob, the right amount is hardly any bear at all.

Mu was pleased, in the quiet way that was the only way Mu had. She'd told him once that everything good comes in a right amount; and here it was again, wearing a beetle. "Gently does it," said Mu, who had fed the whole Wood and never spilt a drop — and Pooh, lifting Bob over a root on the tip of one paw, found that gently did do, and did it rather well.

Then there was the going slowly, which Pooh learnt because Bob went slowly, there being no other speed a beetle has. A whole hour's journey for Bob was about one step for Pooh, so that Pooh was forever arriving first and having to go back for him, and arriving first again, until he saw the sense in going at Bob's pace and calling that the right one. And the funny thing — Pooh told me this himself, long after, and still looked a little surprised — was that going slow was not the dull part before the good part. Going slow was the good part. A bear in a hurry misses very nearly all of the Wood; Bob, who could not have hurried to save his life, missed none of it; and Pooh, keeping Bob's time, found he was missing none of it either.

And the last of it Pooh learnt was that Bob ran out of Bob long before Pooh ran out of wanting to play. A beetle is given only so much beetle for one day, and spends it sooner than you'd think to look at him. Mica was the one who showed Pooh this, being the smallest of them all, and knowing better than any of the big ones what it is to be tired while everyone else is still going strong. She taught him to watch for the very moment Bob's legs began to argue with one another, and to stop the game a little before that and not a little after; because a game stopped too late leaves somebody crumpled in the grass, and a game stopped just in time leaves everybody wanting tomorrow. So Pooh learnt to carry Bob home on the flat of his paw at the end of a long day, very slowly, so as not to joggle him — and Bob would ride along half asleep, and that was a game too, and very probably the best one.

So my bear, who could have flattened the whole Wood and never felt the bump, learnt instead to go through it as if every last thing in it might be a Bob: slow, and careful, and watching for when the small ones tired. The strong are not gentle by accident. They are gentle on purpose, or they are not gentle at all; and that, said Mu, was about the size of it.

Now I must tell about the hunny, because it was Bob's first, and my bear was there for it, which he says is one of the best things he has ever been there for.

It had not occurred to Pooh, until then, that there could be anybody in the Wood who had never tasted hunny. It occurred to him rather suddenly, one afternoon, in the middle of a mouthful; and he stopped, with his paw half in the pot, and looked at Bob for a long while, the way you look at somebody who has been terribly wronged and doesn't know it.

"Bob," he said. "What does hunny taste like?"

"I couldn't say," said Bob. "Leaves, I expect. Most things do."

And Pooh was so sorry for him, and so pleased for what was about to happen to him, that he could hardly get the drop out of the pot fast enough. One drop — for a drop of hunny to a beetle is what a whole pot is to a bear, or very nearly; and Bob was a good while getting round it, and had to sit down twice.

He did not say anything when he had finished. He polished his face for a long time, which among beetles is a speech.

And here is the curious part, and Pooh made me promise to put it in: watching Bob eat that drop was sweeter than eating it himself would have been. He did not see how that could be, since the hunny had gone into Bob and not into him, and sweetness ought to stay where it is put. But there it was. He tried it again the next day, to be sure, and it was sweeter still; and after that Pooh always kept a drop by, in a curled leaf, for Bob's supper. And if you want to know why the Queen made so much hunny and gave the whole of it away, you may work it out from this, for it is the same thing, only smaller. I worked it out myself, in the middle of my bread and jam, and was so pleased I nearly fell off my chair.

Then there was the seeing, which they traded; and I ought to explain it, only my bear explained it to me better, and I am not sure I can do it the way he did.

A beetle's world, you understand, is mostly stems. Grass to a beetle is a forest, and the Forest itself is only a rumour, a thing the grass has opinions about; and Bob had lived the whole of his life at the bottom of everything and had never once seen the top. So one morning Pooh picked him up, and set him on his head, between his ears, where the fur is best for holding on to, and stood up; and Bob saw the Wood.

He was quiet for so long that Pooh thought he had fallen off.

"All that," said Bob at last, "and nobody told me."

And after that they went everywhere in that arrangement — the bear underneath, the beetle on top, like a very large ship with a very small captain — and Bob would call down how things looked from up there, and Pooh would call up how they smelled from down here, and between them they had the whole of it.

But the trade went the other way too, and Pooh always said he got the better half. For Bob knew the underneath of the Wood, the small world at the bottom of the big one, and he showed it to Pooh an inch at a time: the doors in the roots of the old oak, and who lived behind them; the underside of a leaf, which is paler than the top and has all the writing on it; the road the ants keep, which has rules, and traffic, and a police ant on point duty. Pooh had walked over the whole of it all his life and never seen a bit of it. He went about very carefully after that, and looked where he put his paws — which, as you know, he had reason to do anyway.

I asked him once which was better, the top of the Wood or the bottom of it. He thought about it the whole of teatime, and had hunny twice, and said at last that the Wood was best in the middle, where friends are usually found.

# Chapter Four
### *The Night of the Beetle*

It was Pooh who noticed, because Mica had taught him how: by the smallest things first.

The games began stopping earlier. Not much earlier — a beetle's day is a small thing to begin with, and it shrank the way an afternoon shrinks, so gently that you cannot say when it happened, only that the lamps are lit and it is evening. Bob's legs took to arguing with one another before a game had properly begun; and the journeys got shorter, and the rides on Pooh's head got longer, until one day the whole of the game was the ride itself, very slow, under the trees. Pooh did not say anything about it. Bob did not say anything about it either. There is a kind of politeness between friends about such things, which is not the same as pretending; though I am not sure it is always the better way.

So Pooh decided to say something. But not to Bob — you cannot say such a thing to Bob, thought Pooh, when it is Bob the thing is about — so he went and said it to Unda; for Unda was the one who usually questioned this sort of thing.

She was at the shallow end of the lake, being wrong about something to do with reeds. Pooh sat down beside her, the way you sit down when the sitting is most of the message, and Unda left off the reeds at once.

"It's Bob," said Pooh. "The games keep stopping earlier. I count them."

And Unda, who was hardly ever right, was right this time, and told him, kindly and plainly, the way you tell a bear who is old enough to count games: that Bob was not ill, only spending the last of his beetle.

"Every animal is given a certain amount, and no more," she said, "all but us four mothers. We are not from the Wood, you see; we came by the will of Mellea, and that sort of will does not tire. But everything that is born in the Wood belongs to the Wood, and goes back to it. Nobody ends here, Pooh. They sleep, and the sleeping becomes forest. It is why the Wood is so kind, if you think about it. Look what it is made of. But know that a beetle is given less than most, and a first bear more than anybody; and the stopping will go on getting earlier, and earlier, until one evening the game will not stop early. It will stop the way your sand animals stop at night, from which they do not come back. A first bear, with all that bear given to him, will see a great many friends go to their sleep sooner than him; for that is how the Wood lives — by new things coming, and other things becoming part of the forest again, all the time."

Pooh looked. It went a long way to look at the Wood that way, and he never afterwards quite stopped.

"Is there nothing at all to do?" he said at last.

"There is one thing," said Unda. "Not a mending thing. There is no mending for this, not for time. Old age aches, especially in a body as small as that — for there is less room to put the pain into. So the one thing left is to make him feel better. On the far bank of the lake, on the wet side of the willows, there grows a leaf that is the best of all leaves for a tired beetle. It makes the evening comfortable, the way a fire does, or somebody staying. I have seen it from the water. I have never seen anybody fetch it. The far bank is a long way, even for the big ones."

She did not say that the water between was deep past knowing. She did not have to. Pooh had grown up beside that lake, throwing things in to watch them go in; and he knew, better than anybody, how very far down the splash went. Unda explained it to the cub because she knew the little bear's heart: Pooh would do the only thing that was left, which turned out to be the biggest thing there was.

He went the next morning at first light, so that Bob would not see him go and guess; and he told nobody else either, for it seemed to him the sort of thing a friend does by himself.

I shall tell the crossing the way he told it to me, because he told it very honestly; and I think the honest telling is the bravest part.

The lake in the early morning is not the lake of the afternoon. There was mist on it, and no birds, and the far bank was not anywhere to be seen — there was only water, going out and out until it stopped being water you could look at and became water you had to think about. Pooh stood at the edge of it a long time. He thought about going home. He thought about it very thoroughly, twice; and then he thought about Bob's legs arguing before the game had begun.

Up to his knees it was only cold. Past his knees it was cold and serious. And then the bottom of the lake did the thing that bottoms of lakes do, which is to leave, all at once, without saying anything; and there was nothing under Pooh anywhere, and nothing in front of him but grey, and nothing to do but swim. He was not good at it. He was good enough. He swam the way he did everything, slowly and all of a piece, with the far bank taking its time about arriving; and in the very middle, where both shores are only rumours, a lake is bigger than everything, and a bear in it is about the size of a beetle. If he cried a little, out there, the lake kept it to itself.

The far bank arrived at last, after forever. And there were the willows, and the wet side of them, and the leaf, row upon row of it, exactly as Unda had said; and nothing on that bank had ever been so glad to see a leaf as one wet bear was, that morning, very far from home.

He carried a whole bundle back on the top of his head, between his ears, where the fur is best for holding on to; and though the crossing home was just as far, it did not feel it, for the way back with what you came for is always shorter than the way out, since this time you know where you are going. Which ought not to make a difference, but does.

Bob looked at the bundle for a long time. He knew every leaf in the Wood, and he knew this one, and he knew which bank it grew on.

"You went over the water," he said.

"Mm," said Pooh.

"Grande Gratia!" said Bob.

A beetle keeps his words the way he keeps his strength, for when they are truly wanted; so hearing Bob thank him this loudly weighed all the more. Pooh, still wet to the ears, felt warm the whole way through.

Bob took a little of the leaf that evening, and sat very still, and his legs left off arguing for the first time in days.

"Better?" said Pooh.

"A lot, yes," said Bob.

So Pooh stayed with his first friend. He stopped going anywhere much. He brought the Wood to Bob instead, a day at a time — how the morning had smelled, and what the ants were up to, and which of the young trees had done something silly in the wind — and Bob took his leaf, and listened, and put in corrections where the facts wanted them, for he had strong opinions about ants and great knowledge of plants.

And Mica came in the evenings, and sat with them, small and quiet; and it was Mica who told Pooh the last of it. You cannot keep a day, she said. Days are not for keeping. But you can notice one while it is going — that was the whole trick, she said, and most sadness in the world is only not noticing until afterwards. So Pooh learnt to notice the days. Not to hold them, for they will not be held; only to notice them, one at a time.

I had to stop writing here, when my bear told me that part, and go and look at my hunny pot for a while. A hunny pot has a bottom. That is not the sad thing about hunny pots; it is the reason you lick the spoon slowly, and go round the rim twice. I believe that is what Mica meant, or near enough. And when I said so to my bear, he laughed, and had some hunny straight away, and went round the rim twice, to show me that I had got it right.

And one evening, at the end of a good day — it had been a good day; the sun had done everything a sun can do, and the leaf had made the evening comfortable — Bob rode home on the flat of Pooh's head, between his ears, half asleep, the way he liked best.

"Pooh," he said.

"Mm," said Pooh.

"Everyone gets there eventually."

And he said it the way he had said the other thing on the very first day, without much heat, as a thing that is simply true and cannot be helped; only this time it did not make Pooh feel worse. And somewhere under the old oak, between one slow step and the next, Bob got there.

They laid him by the doors in the roots, where he had known everybody; and in the spring there was a new green in that place, very small, very low down, that had not been there before. Pooh used to visit it. He said you could not be sure it was Bob, and you could not be sure it wasn't, and that it seemed the sort of spot Bob would choose, being close to the ground and near his friends.

And the name stayed. It was handed down, smallest to smallest, the way you hand down a coat, so that the Wood was never afterwards without a Robert in it. Bob never knew he had been the first of them. Pooh knew. He was the one who found out.

I asked my bear once whether he still missed Bob, after all the years. He thought about it, and had a little hunny first. "Only on the good days," he said. And when I asked how often that was, he said, "Nearly all of them," and seemed very pleased about it.

# Chapter Five
### *In Which the Wood is Counted*

Now I must tell how counting came to the Wood, because it had not always been there, and somebody had to bring it, and it was a turtle.

Before Tart, the Wood had three amounts, and got along on them well enough. There was a little, and there was a lot, and there was too much; and if you wanted to be very exact about a thing, you could say "a lot, nearly too much," and everyone would nod, and nobody was any the wiser, which suited everybody very well. Bees came in a lot. Rain came in a lot as well, except in the fall, when it came in too much. Trouble came in a little, mostly; and hunny came in never quite enough, whatever amount it came in, if you asked a bear.

The question arrived at the hive, as most of Pooh's questions did. He was sitting under the old tree one morning, watching the bees go about it — out and in, in and out, every one of them busy, every one of them carrying — and the wondering came over him all at once, the way a wondering does: all that buzzing, and all that fetching, day upon day upon day. How much hunny did it come to?

So he counted them. Or he began to. "One," said Pooh, choosing a bee; "two," choosing another; and there his troubles started, because the first bee would not stay being One — it went round the tree and came back looking exactly like Six — and every bee in the Wood, seen from underneath, is the same bee, which is a thing the bees themselves are said to be proud of. He got as far as four. Then a bee landed on his nose, and he could not tell whether it was a new bee or one of the counted ones, and it would not say. So Pooh sat down and finished the count the way bears have always finished it.

"One," he said. "Two. A lot."

And a lot it was, and a lot it stayed, and he was no wiser than when he began, only hungrier.

"Three hundred and forty-one," said a voice, down by his feet.

Pooh looked down. There was a small turtle there, who might have been there an hour or a week — turtles are not much noticed, which they consider one of their advantages — looking up at him with the calm of somebody who has never once been wrong about a number.

"Pardono?" said Pooh.

"Three hundred and forty-one," said the turtle. "Bees. Three hundred and forty in the hive, and one on your nose."

That was Tart. She was young, as turtles go, which is still a good deal older than a bear feels; and she was the first counter there ever was in that Wood. Nobody had given her the work. Some creatures are simply born with a thing to do, the way a bee is born knowing about flowers; and Tart had been born knowing that things come in amounts, and that amounts have names, and that finding the name of an amount is one of the quiet pleasures of the world.

"Is it hard?" said Pooh, meaning the counting.

"It is exact," said Tart; which was not an answer, and was the completest answer she had.

"But they don't hold still," said Pooh. "I had got to four, and four sat on my nose."

"They are not meant to hold still," said Tart. "Bees are one of the trickiest calculations there are."

"What nations?" asked Pooh.

"None," said Tart, and went on. "You cannot count them at the hive, for they will not take turns properly: some pop out and back a dozen times in a morning, and some go off on expeditions so long you would give them up entirely, and so you count them never, or twice, or both. No. For bees you must have a system before you begin."

"Sister who?" inquired Pooh, who was trying his hardest to keep up.

"A system," said Tart. "A system is finding out what is linked to what. Nothing in a hive is only itself: the flowers are linked to the fetchers, and the fetchers to the comb, and the comb to the young ones, and the young ones to next summer's fetchers; and once you understand the connections, the counting is the easy part. So you count the jobs and the bees doing each job, and you add the jobs together at the end; and you must not hurry any part of it, for a hurried system gives you a number that is as good as any guess."

It was at about this point that Tart, without leaving off her explaining, began counting something new; and what she was counting was Pooh. Seven blinks, she made it, and two yawns, and one long look towards the hive that had nothing to do with hunny numbers. She did the sum. And that is why — she told my bear so herself, long after — she decided not to explain the next part, which is how you calculate the drops of hunny from the flowers; for it is the most beautiful sum in the Wood, and she was not going to spend it on a bear who was down to his last blink.

So she skipped to the end, which was the part Pooh wanted anyway; and the end was a wonderful part, and Pooh made her do it twice. For when he asked how much hunny three hundred and forty-one bees came to, Tart did not go and look. She sat quite still, with her eyes half shut, and counted things that were not there. "One bee," she said, "brings in one drop of a drop in a day. Three hundred and forty-one bees" — and her eyes went further shut — "is a comb a week; is thirty pots by the turn of the leaves." And when the turn of the leaves came, Pooh checked, pot by pot, and it was thirty, and he never quite got over it. Counting the bees was clever. But counting pots before there were any pots — counting hunny that was still inside the flowers — that was a magic, and it is called calculating, and Tart could do it lying down.

"Thirty pots," said Pooh, to be quite sure. "Is that a lot?"

"In turtle," said Tart, "it is thirty. In bear, I believe it is a lot, nearly too much."

And Pooh went home very content; for a lot, nearly too much is the happiest amount a bear can hear about hunny, there being no such thing, where hunny is concerned, as too much.

But halfway home the links kept going in his head, all by themselves, out of the hive and into the flowers and up into the sky; and he stopped, and stood a while, and turned round, and came all the way back to say it.

"The rain is in the hunny," said Pooh. "The rain goes into the flowers, and the flowers go into the bees, and the bees put it in the pots. When it rains, it is raining hunny. A little. Eventually."

And Tart looked at him for a long moment, the way you look at a sum that has come out right by a road you never taught it.

"That," she said, "is the first true calculation ever made by a bear." She said it the way she said her numbers — exactly, and without spending more on it than it cost — but Pooh walked on air the whole way home, and ever afterwards he rather liked the rain, even the fall too much kind of rain.

After that the counting got about, the way good things do in a wood. A squirrel came, wanting his nuts counted, but privately, and somewhere the other squirrels were not; and Tart counted them without once saying the number out loud, which is the politest counting there is. Somebody asked whether the stars were the same ones every night, or new ones; Tart said she would want a ladder and a whole winter, and she was given neither, so it is not known to this day. And the bees were informed, by way of courtesy, that there were three hundred and forty-one of them; and it is said they were pleased about it, in their way, and buzzed the number round the meadow all afternoon, as though they had counted it themselves.

I ought to say here that I am forever getting my sums wrong at school; and my bear says I count like a bear and not like a turtle, and that most wrong sums are not dummy sums but only hurried ones. I have tried it the slow way, and they do come out righter. Though I have not told anybody at school about the turtle, because there are things school is not ready for.

And I can tell you how far her counting went, because I had it from Bert. Bert is a turtle, and the first Robert I ever met — which shows you how far that name travelled, going down all those years, smallest to smallest, until it landed on somebody with a shell. Bert says that in her day Tart counted everything worth the trouble: how much hunny a hive makes in a summer, and how many bees there are in one hive, and how many bees there are in all the hives of all the Wood together, which is a number I shall not write down, because you would think I had made it up. And she counted the Wood itself, aker by aker, walking it the slow way, all of one summer and a piece of the next; and it came out at one hundred exactly, which everybody felt was very handsome of the Wood. That is why it is put down as the 100 Aker Wood, with the number written as a number; for the name is the count, and the count was Tart's, and the Wood has kept both ever since.

# Chapter Six
### *In Which Pooh walks into a Web*

Pooh met Ara the way everybody meets a spider, which is suddenly, and with his face.

He was walking between the two hazels one bright morning, thinking about hunny, when the air in front of him turned out not to be air; and there was a moment of silver everywhere, and a tickling, and a sense of having put his whole head through somebody's morning. Pooh stood very still, the way you do when you cannot tell yet how much trouble you are in, and picked the shining threads off his ears one at a time.

"I am so very sorry," he said, to whoever the threads belonged to. "I didn't see it. It was being invisible."

"It was being finished," said a small voice, up and to the left; "which was its mistake, for nothing interesting happens to a finished thing. Hold still, please. You have got the best corner of it on your nose, and I shall want that back."

And down a single thread, on no legs at all as far as Pooh could tell, came a small grey spider, who walked across the air to his nose as though the air were a garden path, gathered up the silk that was there, wound it away somewhere about herself, and looked at him — not crossly; the way a workman looks at weather. That was Ara. She had more legs than anybody in the Wood and less hurry than anybody except Tart; and she was never entirely still, even when she was still, for some part of her was always tending to a thread.

Now Pooh had broken things before — you will remember what he nearly did to Bob, and he had never once forgotten it — and he stood there with his heart starting to go all wrong inside him, for the web had plainly taken the whole of the night, and the whole of the night was gone from the world on account of his face. He began to say so. He got as far as "Your silver thing — " and stopped; because Ara was not listening, and she was not grieving, and she was not looking at the wreck of it at all.

She was weaving.

She had gone straight back to the empty place between the hazels, and the new thread was already out and swinging, and she was away along it with the deep and total contentment of somebody doing the exact thing they are for. Pooh watched her a long while. Then he sat down under the hazels, quietly, so as not to joggle the morning again, and asked the thing he could not get past.

"Don't you mind?"

"Mind what?" said Ara, not stopping.

"Your silver thing. It took you all night. And I wore it on my head."

Ara went out to the end of a spoke and fastened something and came back before she answered; for she did her talking the way she did her weaving, without stopping and without hurry, and a sentence and a thread often got finished together.

"The web," she said, "is where the weaving goes when I have had the good of it. The weaving is the part that is mine. A web is not for keeping, little bear. It is for the spinning of it; and then it is for the flies; and then it is for the dew, which wears it better than I ever did; and then it is for beginning again. If you love the web you have a broken heart every morning of your life. If you love the weaving, nobody can take a thing from you, for the weaving is always exactly where I am."

"Even when I put my face in it?" said Pooh.

"You have been," said Ara, "the biggest fly all week; and the politest. Most of them do not apologise."

And Pooh laughed, though he was never entirely sure why, which as you know is the very best way to laugh; and after that the morning was all right again, and he stayed to watch the making.

The making was the finest thing he had ever watched. Ara worked from the outside in, first the long spokes, pulled taut and tested one by one with a small deliberate tug, the way you test a knot that your whole weight is going to believe in; and then the going round and round, in and in, the sticky spiral laid down as evenly as rings on water; and the whole of it done at a walk, never at a run, and never once undone for being wrong, because it was never wrong, because she never hurried it. Pooh asked how she knew where every thread went.

"I don't," said Ara, going on. "I have been at this the whole of my life, and I am never quite sure where a thread goes until it has gone there. Some mornings I find one that was wrong the whole time, sitting in the web as pleased as anything; and I leave it, and remember it, and I try to do it better in the next web. That is the beauty of a craft, little bear. There is no bottom to it. You can always learn more."

Then, because watching a making always turns into wanting to make, Pooh asked whether he might try; and Ara said that silk was for spiders but that making was for everybody, and set about spinning a thicker sort of silk, bear-weight, winding it as she went; and in a while there were three fat balls of it, which she handed over the way you hand over tools. It went the way you would expect. A bear's paws are mostly pillow, as you know, and pillows are honest workers but poor weavers; and by the middle of the afternoon Pooh had made a thing, slung between two roots, that was mostly knots, with some accidental hunny in between, and one corner that had given up entirely.

Ara came down and inspected it properly, thread by thread, taking her time, the way you inspect the work of somebody you intend to tell the truth to.

"This knot is good," she said at last, touching one of them. "The rest is enthusiasm. But that is where you learn."

And the strange thing — Pooh told me this himself — is that it was the proudest he had felt all year; for she had meant it, both halves of it, and praise with the truth still in it goes further down and stays longer than the other kind. He kept an eye on that knot for the rest of the season, going round by the two roots on purpose, until the rain took it; and when the rain took it he found, a little to his surprise, that he did not mind at all, which meant the lesson had gone in without his noticing, the way all the best ones did.

I must put in here what happened with my sampler, because it is the same story wearing my pinafore. I had been sewing a sampler at school, roses along the border, and the roses went crooked — all of them, and each in a different direction, as though they were looking for a way out — and I unpicked the whole afternoon of it and cried. And when I told my bear, he said that Ara unpicked a web every day of her life and had never once cried, the weaving being the part she was in it for; and I said that school samplers are not webs; and he thought about it and said no, there is less dew on them. Which did not mend the roses. But I began again the next day, trying my best but without minding it so much, and the second roses came out straighter, having got their wandering done in the first ones.

The webs are still there, by the way. Not the same ones — Ara would be the first to say that a web is a morning's citizen only — but webs, in the hazels and the long grass and the corners of things, wherever small careful makers have kept at it; and if you are ever out very early, before the sun has dried the Wood, you can see them all silvered over with dew, many of them, hung out like the Wood's own washing.

I asked my bear once what his own making was, since he does not weave. He thought about it, and had a little hunny while he thought, which I ought to have seen was the answer arriving. "Hunny," he said. "I am learning to eat it." And when I said that surely he knew how by now, he said no, not properly, he had only been at it a little while; which is the beauty of any craft.

# Chapter Seven
### *The First Song*

The first song in the world happened on an evening, and it happened to Bufa, and it took her quite by surprise.

Bufa was a toad, and lived at the soft end of the pond, and had the sort of speaking voice that made you look forward to the singing being different; and it was. Nobody knew it yet, though, Bufa least of all. She had spent that whole day the way she spent every day, sitting in the warm mud being generally pleased about things; and the evening came down golden, and the midges went up in their little towers, and the light did what the light does over water at the end of a good day — and something in Bufa filled up past the top of her, the way a pot fills past the top of itself in the rain, and came out of her mouth.

And it was not a croak.

It went up, and along, and down, and up again further, and it had the evening inside it somehow — the gold, and the midges, and the being pleased — and every creature in that part of the Wood stopped what it was doing. Tart stopped counting, in the very middle of a number, which had never once happened. Ara stopped weaving with a thread still in the air. And Pooh, who was on his way home with a pot, sat down where he was, on a thistle, and did not notice the thistle until the next day.

Nobody had a name for it, because it had never happened before. It was over in a little while, the way evenings are; and Bufa blinked, and looked around at everybody looking at her, and said, in her ordinary voice, which was a disappointment to return to:

"Pardono everyone. I don't know what that was."

That was the first song. And the trouble started the very next day; because the whole Wood came to the soft end of the pond, everybody, wanting it again — and Bufa did not know what they were all doing there. She opened her mouth and croaked, which is a fine sound for a toad but was not what anybody had walked all that way for. They asked, but she did not understand what they wanted. The song was gone. She had not put it anywhere, she explained; it had not seemed like a thing that needed putting; it had gone through her the way a day goes through a Wood, and she remembered that it had happened, and that it had been lovely, and nothing else about it whatsoever.

"Perhaps another will come," said Bufa, and settled into the mud, entirely unworried, being the sort of person who does not keep her songs and is not kept by them either.

But the Wood did not take the losing of the song lying down. A thing that good, everybody felt, ought to be got back; and since nobody knew how you got a song back, they set about it the way you set about anything in a wood, which is all together, and mostly wrong.

First they tried putting the evening back the way it had been, on the theory that the song had come out of it and might again. They walked Bufa to the very same warm mud at the very same golden hour, and stood round her in a large and hopeful circle, watching. The light did its part. Bufa sat in the middle of it all, feeling looked at from every direction, and filled up not one bit, and the more hopeful the circle grew the emptier she felt, until at last she said she was very sorry, but the only thing rising in her that evening was mud.

Then they tried making a song themselves, out of the materials on hand. Tart went first, on the grounds that she had a system: she took the sounds one at a time, in their proper order, at a sensible pace — "one," sang Tart, "two, three, four" — and it was very exact, and you could have set your afternoon by it, and it was not a song; it was counting with its hat on. Ara suggested that Bufa build the next one the way a web is built, outside in, spokes first; and Bufa tried, and produced a croak with architecture, which the Wood agreed was interesting, in the way things are interesting when you are fond of the person who made them. And Pooh went home and tried privately, with a small hunny for company; and what came out was not the song either. It was a small up-and-down sort of noise about hunny, no longer than a spoonful, and it would not turn into anything grander however much hunny he gave it. But he found he liked it. He hummed it the next day without meaning to, and the day after that on purpose; and though it was never a full song, it was his, and a bear with a hum of his own is a bear with company on any road. He kept it all his life.

So the Wood learnt, by trying everything, the thing that trying everything teaches: that a song would not be made the way a count is made, or a web. You could not order it, or build it, or stand round it in a circle being encouraging. Whatever songs were, they were not that kind of making.

It was Pooh who found what kind they were, and he found it the way he found everything, which was sideways, while thinking about something else. He was waiting near the pond a few days later — waiting for the singing, like everybody, though nobody said so — and to pass the time he was listening, the way Mica had taught him, to the small sounds under the big ones. And what he heard, once he had got underneath, was this: that the Wood was full of time. Tart, off on an errand, counted as she went, and her counting had a tick in it. Ara's weaving, when the wind held its breath, went tap and pull, tap and pull. His own chewing, he noticed, with some hunny he happened to have, went in a steady and dignified manner that he had never appreciated before. Everything that happened in the Wood happened in a rhythm; the Wood kept time all day long, and had never once been told to.

So he told Bufa. He said, "Everything is going like this" — and he hummed it, the way he had been humming, but with the rhythm the Wood had that very dusk inside it, the tick of it, the tap and pull — "and I think your song was going like the evening."

And Bufa sat up out of the mud, and listened to the Wood the way Pooh was listening to it; and you could see the filling-up starting again, past the top of her; and that evening, with the whole Wood's time inside her — the counting and the weaving and all the other noises — she sang the second song in the world. It was even better than the first one, having everybody in it; and each creature heard a piece of itself go by — Tart her counting, Ara her tap and pull, the hum of a little bear — so that afterwards nobody could quite say whether Bufa had made the song or the Wood had. The truth is it was both, which is the proper way of making a song together, and the only one anybody has ever found. And by the next morning Bufa had forgotten it just as completely, and was just as pleased.

Now this might have gone on forever — a song a season, and each one lost by sundown the day after, like the finest webs — if it had not been for Lena; because Lena, that second evening, had been listening from the deep water with just her ears out. And Lena forgot nothing. She had riddles in her that were older than some of the trees. And when Bufa woke the next day, empty and cheerful, Lena rose up out of the lake and sang the whole song back to her, note for note, with the counting and the weaving and the humming all in their places; and Bufa listened to her own song the way you would listen to news of a friend who had gone travelling.

"I like that sound," said Bufa, delighted. "Sounds sweet like hunny and feels warm like mud. Wherever did you get it?"

"You left it lying about," said Lena, "in everybody who heard it."

And that was the great discovery, greater even than the singing, though it made less noise: that a song is not kept in the singer. It is kept in whoever heard it; and if enough of them heard it, it cannot be lost at all, no matter how thoroughly the singer forgets, for somebody will always have a piece, and the pieces find each other. Bufa made the songs, and let them go, the way Ara let her webs go. Lena caught them, and kept them, and — being Lena — went one further.

For Lena had spent the whole of her long life teaching things to creatures without letting them notice, and she knew a good travelling cart when she heard one coming. She began to teach the songs to her friends, and singing became her favourite game; and inside the songs, where nobody was looking, she began to put things worth knowing. There was a song about the two mushrooms you must never eat, which was so catchy that no animal in that Wood was ever poisoned again. There was a song for finding your way home, with the landmarks in it in their right order, so that even the smallest and most turned-around of the small folk could sing themselves back to their own doors. And there was a song about a bear who crossed the deep water for a friend; and animals who had never met Pooh, animals at the far ends of the Wood, knew that story and could sing it, which is a strange thing to think about, and a stranger thing to be the bear about.

Lena's songs were the most profound. Mu, of course, sang mostly about food. Everybody liked Unda's, because she kept discovering new tunes, generally by accident. But Mica's songs, which sounded more like a hum than a song, were very important ones; for they were the only ballads of Mellea there ever were, and I have heard some of these hums from my bear.

That was how it began, my bear says — the telling of things. Before the songs, what a creature knew stayed inside that creature, and stopped there. After the songs, what one of them knew, all of them could know; and what all of them knew could not be lost, not even by everybody forgetting a piece of it each. The Wood had been full of time, and Bufa gave the time a voice, and Lena filled the voice with things worth keeping; and knowledge went about from song to song like a pot of hunny passed along a table, getting a little on everybody.

I know these songs are true, by the way, because I have some of the same kind. Nobody at school can say who made the rhymes we skip to, or the one about the crooked man, or the song that tells you your left hand from your right; they have just always been there, going from child to child, with nobody minding them and nobody able to lose them. My bear says that is Lena's trick, still working; and that if a thing truly matters, you should not write it in a book at all — you should hide it in a song and give it to children, which is the safest place anything has ever been kept.

He still has one of Bufa's, you know. The first one. He is the only one left who heard it made, and he hums it sometimes, of an evening, when the light does what the light does over water; and I asked him once what it was about, and he said, "The evening it was born in." And I listened, and it was true; it fit that evening too, and I expect it fits them all, which is why it has lasted. It is a very simple song. It goes up, and along, and down, and up again further. I would write it out for you, but a song will not lie flat on a page; you will have to come and hear him hum it, some evening, at the right sort of hour.

# Chapter Eight
### *In Which the Years Go By*

The years after the first song went by the way the summer holidays go, which is to say that nobody watched them go, and then they were gone. This chapter must go quickly too, for the years went quickly, and my bear says it would be rude to walk where the Wood ran.

The Wood was filling up in those years — with new songs, and mostly with knowing things. For the Mothers never kept their knowing to themselves, the way a girl at my school keeps her best coloured pencils, too good to use, so that nobody uses them, herself included, and they go on being perfect and useless in their tin. But the Mothers gave theirs away daily, the way Mellea gave sweetness, without meaning anything by it; and knowledge that is given away daily stops belonging to anybody, and then it belongs to everybody, which is the only safe place — as Lena could have told them, and in fact had, in a song.

It began at the suppers. There had always been Mu's supper, where nobody ate alone; and then one year there were suppers Mu had not set, at the far ends of the Wood, kept by creatures she had fed when they were small — the same long tables, the same rule of leaving no one out, the same seeing-to-it that the smallest got the warmest place. Mu went to them as a guest, and was fed at tables she had not laid, which is the best thing that can happen to a cook; and so the Wood learnt to feed itself the way it had been fed, which is the whole of what feeding is for.

The finding-out spread the same way. The young ones had watched Unda all their lives, and had learnt the important part, which was not the answers — Unda hardly ever had those — but the going. They said — I don't know yet — the way she had taught the Wood to say it, as a door and not a wall, and off they went; and new things began to be found out all over, more than any one duck could have got round to; the Wood grew more curious each season. Unda was pleased as pleased about every one of them.

The counting spread too. The little ones learnt it from Tart; and they tried to learn some whatnations and a few tried the sisters of Tart as well, but nobody had the patience for those; and Tart said that patience was the first part of a system anyway, so that was that.

And the making — Ara's craft — went furthest of all. It began with knots, as makings do. Ara took her time over the teaching, and she took Lena's help too, for Lena knew better than anybody how teaching is best smuggled; and between them they put the making into games, a game for the tying, and a game of making bridges, and a song about knots and bridges. And the Wood had always lived in what it found, holes and hollows and the undersides of things; and slowly it began to live in what it made, and to make where it lived more like a home. There were bridges over the streams, so that the wet season stopped being a wall; there were houses, up in the trees and beside them, homes more than burrows, with corners that had been thought about; and there were makers in every part of the Wood who had never met Ara at all, and had only met somebody who had. She went about inspecting, thread by thread, taking her time, telling the truth; and — the rest is enthusiasm — became a thing the whole Wood said, long after it had forgotten who said it first.

That was the way of it in those years: the Wood forgot who said things first, because everybody was saying them. It is the same at school — everybody knows which gate sticks, and the trick of jiggling it, and nobody remembers being taught, and nobody ever was. The knowing had gone out of the Mothers and into the ways of the place, where nobody could lose it and nobody could point to it; and if you had asked the Wood, in those years, who was raising it, the Wood would not have understood the question; which is how you know the raising is nearly done.

And somewhere in all those seasons — nobody can point to the one — the Mothers began to be less.

It was not a leaving that anyone saw. Nobody sees that sort of leaving; it is the sort you notice afterwards, and my bear says Mica would want it written down that this is all right, and that noticing afterwards is still noticing. There was a spring when the supper at the old place was kept by the Wood itself, and was a very good supper, and it was only much later that anybody thought to wonder when Mu had last sat at the head of it. There was a summer when the games by the lake went on all season, new ones and old ones, and no great gentle whoosh came up under anybody. There was an autumn when a new thing was found out, right at the far edge of everything, and nobody thought to run and tell Unda, because finding-out belonged to everybody by then. The Wood was so full of what the Mothers had given it that you could not have said, any more, where the Mothers left off and the Wood began; and somewhere inside all that, quietly, they went.

The songs stayed, of course; that is what songs are for. The little ones went on singing Mica's small hums about Mellea, and the counting-songs, and the song of the four mothers. One evening — my bear was there — a very small conilio, which is what they called rabbits, asked who the four mothers had been; and the older ones looked at one another, and the best answer anybody had was to sing the song again. Everybody in the Wood knew the songs. Fewer and fewer had known the weight of Lena rising under them, or the particular warmth of a supper with Mu at the head, or the sound of Unda arriving somewhere upside down; until at last, the way it goes with all first things, the number of those who had known them came down to one bear, who was no longer little, and who did not mind being the last, because he was good at remembering, and it gave him something to do with the good days.

I asked my bear about it, of course, at this point in the writing; I asked where they had gone, and whether they were gone. He had some hunny first, which I ought by now to have known was the answer arriving. He said he had never needed to find them, for he had never lost them. "Where would they be?" I asked, all the same, because I am not Unda's pupil for nothing. "About," he said; and seemed to think that was plenty because he went back to his hunny.

And I have thought about it since, and I believe it is plenty. For I have been in the Wood on many days; and it does feel looked after — fed, and listened to, and found out for, and mended at the corners — only you never see by whom.

# Chapter Nine
### *In Which I Find the Wood*

I ought to begin with how I came into the Wood the first time, and the difficult part is that I do not exactly know. I had played at the edge of the trees the whole of my life, everybody plays at the edge of trees, and the edge is as far as it goes, generally; and then one afternoon, in the summer I was six, the trees went on. That is the whole of the explanation I have. I went in after bluebells, and the bluebells went on, and I went on after them; and when I thought to look behind me, behind me had rather changed; and it is a strange thing, but I do not remember minding.

What I remember is the humming. Somewhere ahead of me there was a small up-and-down sort of noise, no longer than a spoonful, going along very comfortably; and I followed it, because a noise like that is not the noise of anything you would want to walk away from. And I came out into a clearing with an old tree in it, and under the tree was a bear, having his tea.

It was hunny, of course, though I did not know to call it that yet. He was the first bear I had ever seen out of a picture-book, and he was better than the pictures, being rounder, and stickier; and the pictures do not hum. I have wondered since why I was not frightened, and the best I can say is that I was six, and nobody had told me about bears yet; and he says he was not frightened of me either, though nobody had told him about girls at all.

He looked at me the way you look at somebody who might be hungry, which I was, having walked a long way after bluebells; and he held the pot out; and I had some, and it was the best thing I had ever eaten, and I said so. That was the first thing I ever said to him, and he did not understand a word of it.

"Salute," he said.

I know now that this is how the Wood said how-do-you-do in its own old tongue, the one it had spoken since before anything. I did not know it then, so I said how do you do, which he did not know either; and we were both terribly polite, and confused about it, which is a very good way to begin.

Then he told me something which I now think may have been his name. He said it slowly and kindly, twice, the way you pour something out for a small cup so as not to spill it. And I could not take hold of it either time. Because I felt he was giving it to me, I tried to say it back, and what came out made him laugh — not unkindly; he laughs in a way that makes you want to laugh with him — and I tried once more, and it came out different, and worse.

And we laughed some more; but I thought the first part had been his name, so I said mine, hoping to have his back; but he only seemed confused by it.

"Pooh," I said. I do not remember why Pooh. Six does not keep its reasons. And then, because a bear of one's own ought to have a name that cannot get lost, I gave him my own name to go after it; and he was Pooh the Sanders from that afternoon on. And of the two of us, he learnt his new name a great deal faster than I ever learnt his old one; for I never did, and now nobody can say it, and I am sorrier about that than about most things I have done, and he says I am not to be, because a name that will not fit in a friend's mouth is the wrong size, whatever else is right about it. And I think that's why we give pet names to the ones we love.

That was the last conversation we ever had in which nobody understood anybody. He set about learning my words the way he learns everything, sideways, and all at once, and mostly while eating; I would say a thing, and he would have some hunny and say it after me, and by the turn of the leaves he had a great many of my words, and by the next summer the Wood was full of them. For the Wood had always learnt from somebody — it learnt feeding from a supper and counting from a turtle — and it learnt English the way it learnt everything else, by catching it from a friend. My bear asks me to put down on the page that English is a perfectly reasonable language, but that it has no proper word for the outside of a hunny pot, and the old tongue had three.

I love animals of all shapes and sizes, and if you ask me, I would say what my Granny says — the more the merrier. The Wood was filling even then, I think, though I did not know woods well enough to notice. There seemed to be more of everybody each time I came — gophers, and pigs, and mice in quantities, and a family of hedgehogs with no idea of hurrying — and I took it to be the way of woods in summer, the way there are more bees in June than in May, and nobody asks why.

As for getting home, I walked out the way that felt like out, and it was. I was late for tea, but not very; and my mother asked where I had been, and I said after bluebells, which was true; and she asked had I found any, and I had — I had been holding them the whole afternoon, rather squashed from meeting a bear. I pressed them that evening in the big dictionary, and they are in it still, at the page for W, which is nearly at the bottom, so it is a rather good letter to keep flowers flat; and they have kept their colour, which pressed flowers do not always do, and I have my own idea about why, and I am keeping it.

I went back the next day, of course, and the day after that, and all the days a summer is made of; and that is how I came to have my new best friend. At the time I did not know how lucky I was, having a bear for a friend of my own. I thought everybody had one, somewhere, and simply had not mentioned it; and I felt sorry for them later, when I found out.

# Chapter Ten
### *In Which the Wood Learns Its Letters*

School happened to me the year I was seven. It happens to everybody, and there is no sense minding it. What school did not know was that it had a leak. Everything it poured into me on a weekday ran downhill to the Wood by Saturday, homework and all.

I ought to explain about the homework, and the first Saturday I tried to explain to Pooh what homework was — the idea of it, before ever we came to the sums — and it is the only thing I ever saw him fail to understand.

"It is work," I said, "that school gives you to take home."

"Why does the work not stay where it lives?" he asked; which I could not answer.

"They are questions," I tried. "The teacher asks us questions."

"What does she want to know?"

"Nothing. She knows all the answers already."

He thought about that a long while, and had some hunny to help. "Then she is not asking," he said at last. "Asking is when you don't know." And then, hopefully: "And when you have answered them all, does she let you ask one?"

I said that was not how school went; and he nodded, and he offered me some hunny, and never brought the matter up again. After that I did not explain the homework; I only brought it. I would spread it out on the grass between us and be gloomy at it. And Pooh would look at it a long while, and have some hunny, and turn the page the right way up if it wanted it; and then he would fix it. I do not know how. He was not clever the way grown-ups are clever; he did not care about one single thing the sums cared about; but everything seemed to come easily to him once it had been left alone with him a little. The sums went home rested. Their answers came out right in a calm sort of way, as though they had never been worried in the first place; and if there was hunny on the corner of the page, and there generally was, the sums did not seem to mind, and nor did I; and what Miss Thompson thought of the stains, she kept to herself. Pooh never did come to understand what homework was; and he never once got it wrong; and I believe the two things are related.

That is how he learnt his letters — off the edges of my homework, sideways, while fixing it. Nobody taught him, least of all me; he simply had them, after a while, the way you have a path you have walked enough times. And because mine was the only writing he had ever seen close to, he wrote the way I wrote, being seven; and he writes so still. I have seen his G go the wrong way round at exactly the corner where mine used to.

It was the summer the owls came, too — or the summer I noticed them, which in a wood comes to the same thing. They took to the letters at once, and harder than anybody. An owl would sit through a whole afternoon of homework without a word, leaning further and further off its branch; and by the end of that summer there were owls making letters, very solemn and very crooked, and correcting one another's, which is a thing you must love letters a great deal to enjoy. The Wood had never had writing before. The owls behaved as though they had been waiting for it, and had only arrived early. If they were not writing they were off looking for leafy greens to trade with the turtles for their paper sheets, which the turtles were rather good at making; and the papers were mostly blank, except that they had page numbers, which was a thing the turtles liked to put on them, once they learnt that letters can be numbers if you draw them right.

Every season there was more Wood than the season before — more of everybody I knew, and then somebody I didn't: donkeys that year, mild and grey; and voles past counting, which I thought were mice, and they were offended by it; and more pigs. I was happy about it in a proprietary sort of way, the way you are happy when a party fills up, as though I had something to do with it, which of course I hadn't; it is only that the Wood in those years was a very good thing to be near, and everybody seems to have thought of it at once.

The one thing I never forgot of that summer was the day the arithmetic blew away. A wind came up the clearing while the homework lay drying — there had been hunny — and took all four pages at once, each in a different direction, the way pages do, having waited all their lives for it. And the Wood went after them. I had never seen it move all together before: pigs going under things, owls going over them, a donkey pinning one page down with a hoof and looking apologetic about the hoofprint; and Pooh, who does not hurry, walking to the exact spot where the fourth page was going to come down, and waiting for it, and being right. We got them all back. The long division was up the bee tree, and Pooh had to fetch it by the bent-tree way, which was smashing, and the first time I ever saw it; and it came home smelling of hunny rather more than usual; and Miss Thompson, once again, kept her opinion to herself.

While the Wood took up my words, my bear paid me back in his. He taught me that hunny in the old tongue is *mel*, but he kept mine, because it seemed sweeter. He spelt the three words for the outside of a hunny pot, and which one you use when the pot is empty, which is the saddest of the three. And he taught me the word for his own kind, which is Uurso; and I asked where the rest of the Uursos were, since a kind ought to come in more than one; and he looked about him in a considering way, as though the rest of the Uursos might be behind something; and said he would let me know.

He did, too. But that is the next summer's story.

# Chapter Eleven
### *A Night at the Wood*

He let me know on the very first day of the next summer, the way he keeps all his promises, which is by waiting until they keep themselves.

I came into the Wood at the usual place, and there were bears. I had thought, all my life until that morning, that my bear was the only one; and now there were bears the way there are daisies, all at once, as though they had been under the ground the whole time and had only wanted their season — or me wanting them, who knows. Big ones and middling ones, up trees and under them, all of them mild, all of them slow in the pleased sort of way, and every one of them humming something different and nobody minding. And the old word he had taught me stopped being a word with one bear in it; for they were Uursos, all of them, and the Wood said the word now as though it had always had a use for it.

I asked where they had come from. "The way you came," he said; which I could not argue with, for I did not know that either. Nobody else asked. The Wood had room, and now it had bears, and everybody seemed to feel that this was the sort of thing a good summer does.

The smallest of them was Galleon, who was new that spring, and belonged to my bear the way an acorn belongs to an oak. He was made mostly of appetite and stumble, and he attached himself to me on the first afternoon, and I to him, and that was that; I was eight, and he was newer, and between us we had the Wood about right. Where there was hunny he was in it past the elbow, and where there was a game he was under it being trodden on, gladly; and my bear watched him the way the old watch the new, which is with one eye, the other being busy pretending not to.

And it was the summer my bear got his grown-up name. The Wood folk put their heads together about it, quite seriously, in the new language they were all still learning; for a bear with a cub of his own cannot go about being just Pooh; and as everybody knew whose bear he was — since he was always with me — they named him Mr. Sanders, and were pleased with it, and said it often, to practise. I do not think anybody asked him. He stayed Pooh with me; but I felt that Mr. Sanders suited him, for it made him sound like somebody's father, which is what he now was.

It was Galleon who kept me late, the evening I am coming to. There had been a game, and then the finding of a very superior puddle, and then the game again; and summer evenings are tricky, and give you no warning of themselves; and when I looked up at last, the dark was already coming. The dark in a wood does not fall, whatever they say in books. It comes up — out from under the things, first, and then out from between them — and it had always been, of everything there was, the thing I was afraid of. I had never said so in the Wood. There had never been any dark to say it about.

And Galleon, at the first of it, did not look at the dark. He looked at me.

He looked at me the way the new look at the old, especially when you are already eight like me, to find out what sort of thing this was going to be; waiting for my face, so that his face would know what to do. And I felt my face getting ready to teach him to be scared of the dark. And I would not let it.

I do not know how to write what happened then, except that I held my face still and friendly, the way you hold your breath; and something went out of me that had been mine a long time, quietly, the way a held breath goes; and it did not come back. Galleon looked at the dark, and found it good, and went into it after a beetle. And the dark was only the Wood with its eyes closed; the humming was still there, all the hums of it, nearer than in the day; and the owls went over us like slow soft thoughts, for the night is theirs, and they are the gentlest folk in it, and I never once in my life heard an owl hurry.

My bear walked me to the edge of the trees, and said, somewhere along the way, in the voice he keeps for things he is not going to say twice: "The dark cannot teach a cub. Only faces teach. Tonight you taught him nothing — and it is the finest gift he will ever have." That was all. Then I went home down our own lane, which was as dark as the inside of a pocket, and had been, all my life, a thing to be run through with your chest tight; and I walked it slowly, listening; and there was nothing in the dark of my world either, except the world, with its eyes closed.

What other fears I had, went the same way that summer, one at a time, day by day, and each of them, somehow, in front of the cub; until I was a girl with none at all, which I took to be growing up. The Wood had taken my fears the way it took everything — by being itself; and Galleon grew up the bravest of them all, never having learnt from anybody's face that there was another way to be.

I do not know how fear works, even now. For years I said the Wood took my fears — and that is wrong. The Wood took nothing. It could not; the fear was never in the Wood, or in the dark. I escaped it. Or no — I discarded it, for it was within me, in my own head. You can only put it down yourself. I put mine down for Galleon, and I was glad, and I am glad still. Where it went, I never wondered. I suppose it went somewhere. Everything does.

---

# Chapter Twelve
### *In Which the Wood Is Full*

Every season there were more animals than the season before; and I had better say now how far that went, because by the summer I was eleven it had gone past what anybody could call a Wood. It was more. There were bands in it now, proper roaming ones, that came through the far meadows in the morning and were still coming through at tea; and bands of all sorts — and a band in those days was not a single kind of animal, it was whoever happened to be going the same way. The Wood held everybody, and did not seem to get any bigger.

The big kinds came in those years. The Leons came, golden and warm, and did most of their arriving asleep; you would find one in the long grass with pigs dozing against it for the warmth, and nobody woke anybody. The Triggers came, striped and springy, laughing at nearly everything, and bouncing all the time. The Beevers came and went straight to the streams, and built, and where they built the water stood still and deep and polished; and their still ponds were the quietest country in the Wood, and the best for looking at the sky in. And the Jagulars did not exactly arrive; they were suddenly in the trees one summer, and their whole game was letting go of a branch at the moment you went under it, which is alarming only the first time, and after that is a kind of weather. There were otters, with their fancy pockets for picking their favourite rocks. I do remember seeing one sheep, one day; I was told she liked to follow the cows; but I never saw her again. And the elephantes arrived by the ground first — a sort of patient thunder underfoot, a day before them — and then by the treetops moving, and then all at once by themselves, grey and enormous and careful where they put everything, with old kind faces and a way of remembering you between visits, and with some deep knowledge like Lena's, since they were cousins.

By then Pooh knew how much I liked to collect flowers to keep, so he gathered them for me in spring, and every single day some more when he saw me — he had taken it up years before, I cannot say exactly when, the way the best customs cannot say when they started — and he did it every single time, a fistful of whatever different kind he found in the Wood that week, held out without a word; and I ran out of letters in the big dictionary at home, so I started using a couple of my daddy's En-circle-pay-days to flatten all my flowers. There were the suppers, long ones, at the tables the Wood kept going all on its own, where the rule was still the rule: nobody eats alone, and the smallest gets the warmest place. There were the games, which Galleon now ran instead of being trodden on by; he was three that summer, back from his first winter in the north with the other Uursos. And the games were better than ever, for the Triggers had brought bouncing, and bouncing, it turns out, improves nearly everything; and the Beevers' still ponds had given the Wood water games, which everybody played except the Leon cubs, who were the only ones afraid of the water; and everybody let them be, the way you let cats be about rain.

For the Uursos had their custom, and I might as well set it down here, where it belongs among the other customs. They liked the cold end of the year. They went up into it every winter, all of them together, a little further each time, the way you wade a little further each summer; and they came back with the spring, unhurried and pleased. Nobody else in the Wood could see the fun in it. The Uursos did not explain it. It takes a very warm animal, I think, to be fond of the cold.

And there was the telling. That was mine to do. On the slow part of the afternoon they would settle, big ones and small ones anyhow together, and I would tell them about my world; and they collected what I told them the way I pressed flowers, flat and bright and out of season, one per page, and always in heavy books. I told them about bath-tubs, and about umbrellas, and how the postman knocked twice, and how in my world the lions were the kings of the jungle, which was like the Wood but with coconuts in it. The owls asked me about books, and I could never quite explain to them what Encirclepaydays were. I tried; I said that, according to my daddy, it was a book with everything in it; and the owls looked at one another in a way that meant they did not see what the rest of the books were for, in that case, and were too polite to say so. It was Pooh who settled it. He tried the word over once or twice, and found a circle in it, and a paying, and a day; and I told him there was no circle and nothing paid, it was a book, quite square. He had some hunny and thought. "A book with everything in it," he said at last, "won't have room left for the reader." I have thought about it myself on and off for years, and I am not finished either, which I suppose is the room he meant. And I told them about the bell at school, and about my aunt's piano, which had to be listened to on Sundays whether it deserved it or not. Galleon liked the umbrellas best. He asked about the umbrellas most afternoons, and never once believed me.

It was amazing how many animals there were, all playing old games and inventing new ones; and a good thing summer has long days, for even those were not enough. Before the night came up out from under the things, I would start for home, for my mummy was frightened of me coming in late; and I would walk through the dusk slowly, with the humming behind me getting farther and the supper ahead of me getting nearer, which is the best direction there is.

# Chapter Thirteen
### *In Which There Is a Great Deal to Do*

The summer I was twelve there was a great deal to do. There had always been a great deal to do; but it had used to be the Wood's great deal, and now it was me. There was school, which had grown serious about itself; and there were the girls I knew, who had a great deal to do also, and needed me for it; and there was my hair, which had suddenly to be considered, and considered again, and was never once settled all summer.

I came when I could. I want that written plainly, because it is true: I came when I could. Only *could* had grown smaller that year, the way jerseys do; which is to say that it was I who had grown, and nobody's fault.

The Wood thought the hair very fine. I came one day with it braided a new way, and the news went round, and they inspected it with the solemnity they keep for serious matters, one after another, the smallest ones twice; and a Trigger asked whether it had been difficult, and I said it had taken the whole of Sunday, and everybody nodded respectfully, and Galleon said that next summer he might get one. I tried as well to explain about make-up, which the big girls at school had begun to speak of importantly, the way you speak of a country you are going to. Pooh listened all the way through, eating hunny all the while; and then he asked, politely, what it was I had made. And I had to own that I had not made any make-up yet; and he nodded, and said that the best things were slow to make.

Everything else was exactly as it always was, only now I had to catch it up instead of being in it from the start. I would come after a fortnight away, and there would be a new game running, and songs I did not know, which had never happened before; and I learnt them, and it was easy, and nothing was any different, except that the Wood had made them without me. Galleon ran everything that summer; he was four, and had opinions, and the opinions were good ones. The suppers went on, and the flowers came to me the moment I stepped in under the trees, a fistful of whatever the week was doing, held out without a word, as though I had never been gone at all; and the Wood got on beautifully without me, which is what you want; and I want it written that I was glad.

It was that summer I first saw Pooh writing; and he looked very Mr. Sanders doing it. I came in quietly one afternoon, and he was under the old tree with a book of his own — a proper one, of the turtles' paper, page numbers and all — writing in it, slowly, with his tongue out a little, which is how I write too. I did not ask. You may ask a person what they are eating, but never what they are writing; that is a rule I have always felt without being told. I only saw that his G still went the wrong way round, at exactly the corner where mine used to; and I was glad of that too.

It was on one of the last afternoons, with the leaves just thinking about turning, that he told me about the expotition. The winds were changing, he said; and he said it the way he says things about weather, which is the way other people say things about the time of day. The Uursos would go north for the winter as they always went; only this year they would go further — the furthest yet — the biggest expotition to the north there had ever been. Expotition was his word. I never corrected it, though I knew the correct one by now; for expotition was plainly the right word for the thing. All the Uursos would go, the big ones and the small ones and the ones with opinions; and Galleon was wild about it, and had already begun packing, in the way of somebody who has nothing to pack.

I asked what they would find, so far up. He had some hunny, and said you never knew until you went, which was the point of going; and I said I should hear all about it in the spring.

# THE LAY OF FANGORN

*Here begins the second hand. It is not the hand of Sallie Sanders, whose writing goes before. Of its author nothing is known: not the name, not the kind, not the year of the writing. The pages are prose, and yet they were found titled a Lay; it may be that there was once a song, and that this is what remained when the song was gone. Once, and once only, these pages call the war the Second; and they do not say why; and no other writing that survives says anything at all. The pages were found bound together with the Book of Hunny, and none can say who bound them.*

# Part One
### *In Which the Wood Waits*

## I. Of the Departure

In the last year of the Blossoming the Girl came no more to the Wood.

She did not say farewell, for she did not know that she was going; that is the way of children, who leave a place a thousand times and then once. The animals waited at the edge of the trees through the turning of the leaves, and through the falling of the leaves, and through the snow. The conilios, who kept the calendar and the counting of days and such things, said she would come with the spring. The spring came, but Sallie Sanders did not. The flowers bloomed, and there was no sight of the girl who had loved to gather them, and give them to the animals.

While she walked among them there had been a measure in all things. The kinds were many beyond all later counting, and yet the Wood held them as a hand holds water, and did not spill. The wise said afterward that the measure was hers; that the Wood was full because her heart was full, and that so many kinds had never before dwelt together, nor ever would again. But this was said afterward. In those days none knew what kept the peace, and so none knew that it could be lost.

---

## II. Of the Vigil

Seasons came and went. By the second spring the conilios had grown so sure in their reckonings that they named the very day on which Geomy the gopher would put his head above the ground; and on that very day he did, and the Wood marvelled, and the conilios were proud, for it is no small thing to know the calendar so well that the earth itself keeps your appointments. But no reckoning named the day of the Girl; and when spring upon spring had come true in every particular but the one that mattered, it was plain to the conilios that the spring would not bring her of itself, and that the Wood must help her home.

So the idea was the conilios'; but at the assembly of the Wood it was the Leons who first offered to take the lead. And companies were formed of animals of every kind together, to walk the roads of the Wood and light them: fires at the edge of the trees, lit at dusk and fed till morning, so that if she were looking for the way back, the way back would be looking for her also. The conilios drew up the schedules and reckoned the turns; they ordered the wood-piles and the relief of the watches; they made of the waiting a working thing, as their kind makes a working thing of everything it grieves. Every company took its turn at the fires, but the ordering of the whole was conilio work, and was done well.

The companies of the torch-bearers were called the **brandas**, after the word for a torch in the old tongue; and the name was a gentle name then, and it is right that this be written down, because of what was done with the name afterward. And the brandas were mixed of every kind, and loyal within themselves, and each grew skilled in its own manner of hoping. The **Rupperts**, who were beevers for the most part, taught the others of their company the building of watch-towers. The **Abotts** — conilios and triggers mostly, who loved to leap and to hop as those kinds do — learned to write words of light upon the dark with their torches, that Sallie might read them from however far away she was; though the founders of the Abotts, the legend notes, were two pigs, and no one knows why, and it is usually the way of such things.

But hope, when it must wait, builds itself a house; and the house of hope is a committee. There were rosters to be kept, and turns to be argued, and disputes between the companies over whose fires burned at the best crossings; and so the companies sent elders to speak for them, and the elders began to meet whether there was business or not, and the meetings took a name — the **Councils**, as such things are always named. They sat in the great burrow of a conilio, it being the largest and the driest, and so anyone who took part in that ordering of things and of thoughts was called a **Burrowcrat**; and the manner of working that grew up among them, all rosters and rulings and turns to speak, came in time to be known as **Burrowcracy**, after the hole it was hatched in. Who knew how much paperwork and debate were needed to show the way home to one small girl. Thus the Wood came to be slowly governed, by the slow hardening of a vigil into an institution. The Burrowcrats and the Brandas were the children of the waiting. They never afterward remembered their mother.

The years passed. The fires burned. She did not come.

And the fear that the Girl had lost in the Wood, and forgotten — the fear the Wood had taught her to be free of, which had fled from her and found in all that time no place to rest — found a place at last in the growing worry of the animals. But it was a cunning thing, and would not be noticed; so it went first into only a few, and very deep, down where the gentlest feelings are kept, where no one thinks to look for it.

---

## III. Of Troki and the Trotskee

There was in those years a Leon called **Troki**, and the legend must speak carefully of him, for he was the best of his kind and the first of the dead.

Troki held that the Departure was not a fate but a problem; that what could be understood could be undone; that if the Wood could learn *why* she had gone, the Wood would know how to bring her back. He took this doctrine to the owls, who were the lettered folk and kept the records, and together they made a work of it: gatherings at the great oak where the owls had their library, readings of every record of her years among them, theories built and broken and built again. The branda took his name, as brandas did, and was called the **Trotskee**; and like all the brandas of those years it was mixed in kind — owls for the most part, for the work wanted reading, but with Troki the Leon at its head, and a few donkeys, and one elephante named Robert, whom they called **Bobby** to keep him distinct, Robert being a common name among the animals. The Trotskee was the highest of the brandas and the proudest, for it was the only company that could not only read, but stay awake while reading. It was the first of the torch-companies to carry its fire indoors, to the study of the question rather than the lighting of the way; and for a time it was the noblest thing in the Wood, and the oak on those evenings was the brightest tree in it.

But a search for a cause, if it goes long enough unanswered, becomes a search for a culprit. It happened here as it happens everywhere: by small steps, each one reasonable. If she left, something made her leave. If something made her leave, someone permitted it. If someone permitted it, someone is to blame; and blame, unlike the Girl, could always be found. The doctrine of the return grew a shadow, and the shadow grew faster than the doctrine.

And in those same years another Leon was listening, and saying little, and remembering one thing above all the records of the owls. For it was set down — a small thing, said once, the way children say things — that the Girl had told the animals of her own world, and had said that *in her world, the lions were the kings of the jungle.*

She had said it of another country, lightly, and forgotten it by supper. **Regulus** did not forget it. Of all the thousand thousand words of hers in the library of the oak, he carried away that one sentence; and in after years it was the founding text of everything he built, and he never quoted any other, and he never needed to. *She said it.* The only scripture of the regime that destroyed her wood was a true quotation, complete except for the world it belonged to.

---

## IV. Of the Burning of the Oak

On a night in the ninth year of the Departure, the great oak burned.

It burned with the library in it, and with the most part of the records of her years — not all, but most, and the most that mattered; and it burned with Troki in it, who was working late among the records, as he always was. By morning there was nothing of the brightest tree in the Wood but a standing black ruin, and the Wood gathered around it in a grief it had not felt since the Departure itself.

The Councils sealed the ruin of the oak that same morning, pending inquiry. The Burrowcrats took council over it as they took council over everything, in the great burrow, with rosters and rulings and turns to speak; and they reasoned — and the reasoning was sound, which is the bitterest part — that the guilty are drawn back to the place of their guilt, and that whoever came creeping to the ashes would name himself by coming. So a watch was set upon the ruin, and a board was posted upon it, in the fine legal letters of the owls' own school:

> **TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED**

And from that board the ruin took the name it bears in all these pages: the Trespassers Oak.

The inquiry was never closed; in after years it was never meant to be, for a culprit found is a crisis ended, and no one has ever governed long without a crisis. The trap stood baited from that morning on, and caught nothing, ever; for the one it was built to catch had no need to creep back to the ashes — he passed them daily, at his ease, in the open, as a mourner. The only forbidden ground in all the Wood was the one place that held the ashes of whatever the truth had been; and the board that forbade it was written in the letters of the folk the truth would be blamed on.

On the next day the whole Wood took assembly, every kind of it, in a grief that wanted only a direction; and before the assembly came Regulus, and he came as that mourner, and he carried a testimony. He had sat in counsel with Troki, he said, on the very eve of the fire — the two Leons alone, as kin of one kind will sit — and Troki had told him a thing not yet told to any other: that the work was finished. That he had found it at last, the way to bring her home. Or so Regulus said. There had been no other ears at that counsel, and the one who could have gainsaid it was ash.

Then the Wood, which had lost a Leon, learned from Regulus that it had lost the Girl a second time; and grief of that size must be given someone to carry it. Regulus gave it the owls. Who else held the keys of the library? Who else knew what the books contained? And — he asked it gently, as one grieved to be asking — who stood to lose the most by her returning? For the Wood had already begun to forget what the legend here sets straight: that the Trotskee had been mixed of many kinds, that donkeys had sat in it, that Bobby the elephante had carried its lamps. The Wood remembered only that it was owls, mostly, who could read; owls, mostly, who kept the books; owls who were the highest branda and the proudest — and that the coming of Sallie would have ended their importance in an afternoon. The Wood heard this, and the Wood remembered something true, which is how the best lies are built: that everyone had always been bored when the owls spoke of the letters contained in their books; that everyone had nodded, and many had slept, through the readings; that the owls had treasured an importance no one else had ever quite seen the use of. Resentment lay ready, like dry wood. Regulus had only to set his testimony to it.

So the whole Wood came suspicious of the owls; and the legend, which is written in letters by a hand long after, will not pretend the suspicion was reasoned. It was only ready.

Whose paw held the brand that night, no page of these records tells, and no witness survived the telling. The legend does not accuse. The legend counts; and the count of that one night is this: by morning Regulus was rid of the only Leon the Wood loved better than it feared him; he stood armed with the whole Wood's grief; and before him sat the Councils, holding an emergency that no Council in the history of councils has ever had the courage to refuse. Whether fortune gave him these things, or he took them, these writings do not say. It is to be marked only that fortune never gave him anything again; for after that night, he took everything himself.

And to the Councils, in the depth of the mourning, Regulus made his offer, and it was a masterwork, for it asked for everything and sounded like sacrifice. He had understood, he said, perhaps the half of what Troki had solved that last evening — and he said it in lettered language, with the smell of books upon it, though he understood little. But he understood enough to know that with what writings remained in the Wood, the work might be built again. Therefore let every book of the owls be gathered up and carried to the oak, where the work had lived; and let him alone be suffered to enter there, and continue what Troki had begun — for if the burning had been set to destroy the way back to her, then no owl, and no animal untrusted, must ever again come near what books were left. For this the Wood named him **Regent of the Trespassers Oak**: keeper of what remained of the way back to Sallie Sanders, *until she returns*. It was a small office, in words; it was one tree. But the key to that one tree was the key to the only door in the Wood that no one else might open; and much can be grown from one tree, by a patient gardener. And who was to doubt the regent of the return? *Until she returns* became *forever* on no day that anyone could afterward point to; and it was done, as it is always done, with the words of the thing it was killing.

# Part Two
### *In Which Robert Takes a Long Flight*

## V. Of the Suspicion

Then came the years of the question, and the question was: *who do you suspect?*

The Regent's testimony had planted the suspicion; the years grew it. The owls had been Troki's closest circle, the keepers of the burned records, the readers and the writers; and upon them the suspicion settled and stayed, fed from above and watered from below. Had they not been closest to him? Did they not keep their letters and their books — and what did honest folk of the Wood ever need reading for, anyway? What had been in those shelves, that someone wanted burned — unless the owls themselves had burned it, to hide what it told, and with it the one who had uncovered what they kept shielded behind their a's and b's and c's? The questions did not need answers. Questions of that kind are not asked to be answered; they are asked to be repeated.

At the first the Regent brought elders into the Trespassers Oak, to stand among the ashes and the gathered books, and hear what he was finding there. But in time he brought mostly the young, and the credulous; for he found them quicker to understand what he was understanding of the secret scheme of the owls. The elders asked questions. The young asked him to go on.

And the suspicion became the coin of loyalty. To doubt the owls was to love the Girl; to trust an owl was to spit on her memory; and any who said aloud that the questions did not hold together found the questions suddenly asked of *them*. Those who would not suspect were named, with a curl of the lip, **grege** — after the word for a herd in the old tongue: herd-beasts, flock-followers, the trusting and the led — for what else do you call the ones who believe the readers of books over the grief of the Wood? It was an insult, and it was meant to end the argument, and for a while it did.

But the legend sets down what the regime never understood: the owls kept records, and records do not grieve, and do not forget, and do not change their story in the retelling. And quietly, in burrow and thicket, one animal at a time, some of the Wood began to do the unforgivable thing. They began to believe the owls. And the insult, passing from mouth to mouth, fastened not on the cowardly but on the unconvinced, until *grege* meant simply: those who had looked at the regency and seen it. The animals of the after-years bore the name with honour, and it is the name this legend gives them; but let it be remembered that it was coined as a sneer, by the comfortable, against the clear-sighted.

For suspicion eats everything that can be suspected; and the old brandas, mixed of every kind, dissolved one by one, each kind drawing back among its own, until the animals of the Wood kept company only with their kin. The Wood had not always sorted itself by kind. It was taught to.

The brandas that remained, in those same years, ceased to carry torches for her returning. They carried them now in companies, by night, past the houses of the suspected, that the suspected might see the light under the door and understand. The work of hope had become the work of warning. The name was kept. The name made it worse.

---

## VI. Of the Dominion of the Fang

What followed has a long telling and a short one, and the legend gives the short one, for the steps are always the same and the reader of any age will know them.

The Regent's peace required guarding; the guard required strength; the strength gathered to it the strong. The Leons held the lawns under Regulus, who was not the largest of his kind nor the eldest, but the most cunning, and among Leons that is the only crown there is — and let none say the Rex could not fight, for the legend will come to the proof of it. The **Triggers** ran beneath his banner, striped and laughing, and their captain was **Ferox**, of whom it is written that he was never once afraid, and never once merciful. With them went the Weasels, who were small and went unseen, and the Jagulars, who waited in the trees and let go. These were the brandas now — and the leading of the brandas had been Leon work since the first fires — the torch-companies of the vigil, remade as the war-bands of the Fang, bearing the old name of hope as a weapon bears a flag.

Here the legend sets down what the Wood in all its after years never liked to say aloud: that in the Blossoming, and in all the time before it, no creature of the Wood had ever eaten any other. The fang was for show and the claw was for climbing; the measure had held in this also. What broke it was not hunger. It was an argument.

For the owls, who were slandered and shunned and had nothing left but their letters, stood up at last in the open assembly to defend themselves with words, which was the only weapon their kind had ever carried; and the words were good, and the Wood was listening, and the Leons were losing the argument. And tooth and fang ended what reason could not. The first flesh ever tasted in the Wood was tasted that day, in the open, in the middle of a debate; and the Wood looked away, and forgave it, and the legend records the two forgivenesses as they were spoken: *they were the traitors of the Wood* — and, said more quietly, and weighing more, *they were the only ones with feathers among them.* The first to be eaten are the ones who look least like the eaters.

But an argument is not a body, and cannot be eaten. The words of the owls had been sound, and sound words have this property, that they go on bouncing in a wood long after the voice is gone; and many an animal walking home from that assembly found the argument arriving again in its ear, and again at the supper, and again in the night, returning as echoes return, each time fainter and each time harder to answer. The Leons had won the day and lost something they could not name; for from that day there were those in the Wood who doubted what the Leons said — quietly, and to no one, but the doubt was seeded, and doubt of that kind does not need tending. It only needs time.

It was after that day that the board upon the Trespassers Oak was changed. Claws were drawn through the fine word of the law, and over it, scored deep in the crooked letters of the Triggers, who wrote by ear and by claw and spelled as the hungry spell, was nailed the new decree:

> **TRESPASSERS WILL BE EAT ON SITE**

Two hands on one board: the school-letters of the Councils beneath, the claw-letters of the Fang above. The whole history of the regency is written on that board — the law not repealed but written over, in worse spelling and to the same end; and the board changed when the first owl was eaten, and told the truth of the new order to any who could still read. What became of the board in the long years after is a small story, and a strange one, and it is not told here.

Then the hunting began. The owls were taken wherever paw could reach them — and it was the pawed kinds that did the taking, all of them and only them, for the new decree asked nothing of the hoof. So the owls were broken as a tribe: such of their books as had been hidden from the gathering were burned in the open when found, as the oak library had been burned in the dark; and thus the office created so that no book should ever again be lost came, in the end, to burn more books than the fire ever had. Their letters were made a mark of treason. Those that remained kept their learning the way embers are kept, banked and hidden and handed on; and the legend owes its own letters to that keeping, and says so here.

Then the donkeys did the bravest plain thing in all this history: they stood between. They had no letters and no theories and no part in any quarrel; they saw a kind being hunted, and they put themselves in the way of it, because it was wrong, and that was the whole of their reasoning, and it was enough. For this they were chased in their turn; and theirs was the worst portion of all, for the brandas hunted the owls for treason, as they said, but the donkeys they hunted for sport — not for treason, nor for reason, nor for hunger. The donkey's only weapon is the kick of his heels, and so the sport was this: to take the tail before the heels could answer. Many tails hung in the lawns of the Leons. In the **boggy land in the south-east** the donkeys were penned for it; and there, little by little, they were unstuffed — drawn out of themselves, handful by handful, until not enough remained within them, and they died hollow. And the bog where it was done is a gloomy place, and it is gloomy still, and shall be while the Wood remembers, though the Wood no longer remembers why.

---

## VII. Of the Rising of the Grege

At the hunting of the donkeys, the Wood that was left stood up; and thus the Second War began.

The most appalled of all were the elephantes, and the legend gives their reason as it was given, for it is not a reason that a maker of legends would dare to invent: *they share a similar tail.* The greatest kind in the Wood looked at the smallest of the persecutions — a tufted tail taken for sport — and saw its own. And **Herbert Hefferson**, eldest of the elephantes, called Old Hefferson, who had counselled patience through every slander and every burning, turned at last and began to walk toward the lawns of the Leons; and the greges saw which way the wisest and slowest to wrath was walking, and followed.

And to the elephantes came the conilios — the reckoners, the list-makers, the founders of the first brandas — and gave to the rising what they had once given to the vigil: rosters, and stores, and order. Among them was one braver than all his kindred, whose name was also Robert — for Robert, as has been said, is a common name among the animals — and to keep him distinct from the other Roberts of this legend, and from one above all, of whom it has much yet to tell, the grege called him **Rabbie**, in the manner of the north. It was Rabbie who drowned the warrens before the Weasels could come down them, and brought his kindred up out of the dark still fighting. The owls went also to the war, who had never borne arms and had the most to avenge; they fought as owls fight, by night and in silence, and the brandas learned to fear the quiet places under the moon.

Now the legend sets down a thing that the brandas never confessed and the grege never knew: the brandas were afraid. They had seen the size of the elephantes, and now they saw the order of the conilios joined to it, and in secret they were afraid; and fear in the cruel does not counsel retreat; it counsels more cruelty, for aggression is the compensation of the frightened. So the war grew worse than the persecution had been; and whatever had been pure in the brandas once — and something had been, for theirs were the paws that carried the first torches of the vigil — was lost in this way, and is always lost in this way: not at a stroke, but in the compensations of fear.

At the **Six Pine Trees** the brandas dug pits for the elephantes, and covered them with branches, and the great grey ones went down into the dark with their children. The pits remain.

Yet the grege lost, and lost, and lost. The legend of those years is a list of crossings given up and hedgerows burned, and the list is long, and it is not set down here, for the dead do not grow more honoured by the counting.

The grege were driven at last to the river, which was their wall and their trap. Beyond the river the **Beevers** had their lodges and their great dams, and the still water above the dams was the last safe country in all the Wood. There the remnant of every kind gathered, and the water stood between them and the Fang.

It is said that in that camp an old sow asked: *Is there no one in the world but us and them?*

And an owl answered: *There are the Uursos, in the North.*

---

## VIII. Of the Flight of Robert

Of all that was done in the war, the legend holds one deed highest, and it was not done with tooth or heel.

For it was known, in the way that far things are known, that in the North, at the very top of the world, dwelt the **Uursos**: the hunny-eaters, who take no flesh of any creature, and were therefore in no quarrel, and knew of none. Mighty beyond all the kinds of the Wood, and mild beyond all the kinds of the Wood, and far away. And of all the clans of the Uursos the quietest and the wisest was the clan of the **Pooh**.

**Robert the owl** flew in the black of winter, and he flew as the hunted fly, for his kind were proscribed and his errand was treason twice over: once against the Regent's law, which forbade the owls their wings beyond the Wood, and once against the Regent's deepest need, which was that the world outside should never hear. He flew over the lawns of the Leons by night, and over the leaping-trees of the Jagulars by day, and then over no trees at all, into the white, where an owl's wings are a poor argument against the wind. How many days he flew, none can say. The North does not count days in winter.

He came to the Pole at the end of his strength. The Uursos did not make him wait, for they do not make anyone wait; but there was no need of waiting and no need of words. **Sanders the Pooh**, Counsellor of the Uursos, looked up and saw an owl arriving at the top of the world in the dead of winter, and knew in that moment that something had gone wrong in the Hundred Acres, for no bird crosses the white for a small reason. An owl at the Pole in midwinter is itself the whole of the news.

What Robert said, or whether he had breath left to say anything at all, the legend does not know, and will not invent. He died among the Uursos before the morning, of the journey itself, which had been killing him for days and which he had outflown only so far.

They buried him at the top of the world, sitting upright, facing south, the way he had come.

---

## IX. Of the March from the Pole

The Uursos did not take counsel for long, for among them deciding and doing are neighbours.

They planted their standard at the Pole — a single pole of wood, bare, after the plainness of their people — to say to any who came after: *we dwelt here.* And then the Uursos went south, all of them, every one, and the North was empty behind them, and the snow came south that year as if it followed them.

At their head went **Galleon the Pooh, son of Mr. Sanders**, head of his clan and in those years the strong arm of all the Uursos: of whom it was said that the mildness of his people had been given to him entire, and also all the wrath they had never used. And beside him, for counsel, went Sanders the Old, who alone of all things living had seen the Wood that was before the Wood.

# Part Three
### *In Which The Poohs Stay*

## X. Of the Battle of the Ford

The brandas met them at the river, at the wide shallow crossing, for Regulus held that no army crosses a ford against a holding foe. In this he reasoned rightly about every kind but one.

The Uursos did not skirmish, and did not feint, and did not wait for morning. They came down to the water in the grey of the dawn and walked into it, and the river that had been the grege's wall became a road. What befell in the shallows the legend tells shortly: the Triggers broke first, who had never broken, and after them the rest.

It was the first victory of the war, and the grege upon the far bank saw it, and the sound they made was not like cheering. It was more like weeping, the wise say, for hope hurts worst when it arrives.

Of Mr. Sanders the Counsellor, the legend says that he marched, and that he did not fight. What he did in those years, these pages do not tell, and it may be that this silence is his, and was the price of all that he told afterward. The legend keeps his silence with him.

---

## XI. Of the Trap at the Dams

Regulus had lost a battle, and the legend gives him this: he understood at once what manner of war had now come against him, and that it could not be won with teeth. So he set himself to win it with the weapon that had won him the Wood in the first place, which was deceit; and what he made of it the captains of after ages would study, if any age after had kept captains.

In the dark before a certain dawn the brandas fell upon the lodges of the Beevers and took the great dams, swiftly and at small cost, for the Beevers were builders and not soldiers. And then Regulus did the thing that none could read: he set his brandas to dig, and they dug trenches in the low meadow beneath the dams, and got down into them, and waited.

To every eye it was folly. An army in trenches in low ground, where the elephantes had only to come on and tread them into the mud — the brandas had given up the height, the open lawn, every advantage their kind had ever held. The grege looked on it and saw what they were meant to see: the Fang gone to ground, afraid since the Ford, holding a position that could not be held.

And the grege were proud, for they had tasted one victory after years of none, and pride is hungriest in those who have fasted. Proudest of all were the conilios, for they had made the calculations and drawn the plans, and their figures showed great advantage in the ground the brandas had chosen, the more so if the elephantes and the donkeys went at the front. The elephantes hung back, for they felt something amiss in it; and the conilios said that this was fear, and that figures do not feel. Then Old Hefferson spoke, and his words were kept: *"We will go first, and without fear, for we are ready to spend what must be spent to stop this madness, so that we may all go back to doing what animals do when they are not hunting one another. But something tells me there is fog in this clear plan of yours."* And they went first anyway — Old Hefferson and the elephantes at the front, the herds behind — down into the low meadow, against the trenches.

The vanguard of the brandas broke at the first shock and fled, swift and ragged, as routed soldiers flee. It was the finest piece of soldiery in all that war, for not one of the grege doubted it. They poured after, down into the trench-lines and the low ground, kind upon kind, until almost the whole strength of the grege stood crowded in the meadow beneath the dams.

Then the Weasels, who had been at the keys of the dams since the dark before dawn, let the still water remember that it was a flood.

Of the deaths of that morning the legend gives no number, for none was ever made. The water came down without malice and without mercy, as water does, into ground that had been dug deep on purpose to hold it. Old Hefferson was seen lifting the drowning onto his back until the water stood above his own, and was not seen after. The Beevers, who broke from their captors and dove into the breaking of their own work to hold it with their bodies, were not seen in the Wood again, then or ever. Their lodges stand empty above the meadows, and the meadows flood to this day in the heavy rains, for the dams were never built again; there was no one left who knew the building of them. It was the second time a reckoning of the conilios had come true in every particular but the one that mattered.

That ground is called the Floody Place by those who live there now, and they say it as children say it, lightly. Let them. The water has carried the rest away.

## XII. Of the Fall of Regulus

…and so by the high clearing at the top of the Wood the last strength of the Fang was gathered, and against it came up what remained of the grege, and at their head went Galleon the Pooh.

Regulus had measured him from afar, as the cunning do, and read him rightly: that the war now stood upon one Uurso, and that to bring him down was to bring down the heart of all the rest. So he forged the blade. He sent **Ferox**, and with him the whole Royal Guard of the Leons, picked fighters every one, with this charge only: *cut him from his folk, and finish him.*

They did cut him from his folk, on the slope below the clearing, a wall of the Guard between Galleon and all aid. And there the legend tells what the Guard was worth: it was worth some hours of the morning. Ferox came against him last, unafraid as ever, and unafraid he died. But every wound of that morning Galleon carried up the hill with him.

At the top, in the level green among the pines, Regulus was waiting; and let none say the Rex would not fight, for he fought then, and the legend gives him this also: it was the greatest duel that the Wood ever held or ever shall, the most cunning of the Leons whole and fresh against the mightiest of the Uursos bleeding from a morning of battle against the best of the brandas — and the Rex with all his craft and all his strength was not enough.

**Regulus fell, and the fang of him broke on the ground, and the regency that was to last until she returned was finished in the time it takes to say so.**

Galleon stood above him a little while. It is remembered that he asked for hunny; and it was winter, and there was none. Then he went down upon the green beside his enemy, and did not rise. He had spent the whole of himself to save the Wood.

When the Rex was seen to fall, the brandas did not surrender, for surrender must be offered to someone, and they offered nothing. They fled — east, and south, and out of all knowledge — and the Wood did not pursue.

The Uursos laid Galleon on the hill where he fell, among the pines. And this is set down as truth, witnessed by all who stood there: that bees came to that high place at his burial, in the dead of winter, when no bee flies; and none could say from whence they came; and they stayed upon him a while, and went.

Where he lies there grows the one patch of daisies in all that high clearing, the friendliest of flowers; and from that flower, the Poohs say, comes the best hunny in the Wood. The pines of that clearing stand round about in a ring, and they cannot be counted — some say three-and-sixty, some four-and-sixty, and no two countings agree — for they stand as mourners stand, and will not be numbered.

And the Wood, which had no monuments left to raise and no craft left to raise them, did the one thing that the poorest survivors can always still do: it made a song. It was sung first at the burial, and then at the fires, and then wherever two of the grege met; and it was called **Galleon's Lay**. The song is lost, as songs are lost — it was never written, for the writers were too few and the singers thought it would be sung forever. But the name of the song fastened to the ground where it was first sung, and the ground has kept it; only the mouths of the young, year wearing upon year, have rubbed the word smooth, the way a stair-stone is rubbed by feet, until *Lay* became *Lap*. The place is called Galleon's Lap to this day, by those who go up there when something solemn is to be done, and feel that the ground asks it of them, and cannot say why.

It may be that these pages were given their title in memory of that lost song. The legend does not know. The legend is prose, and knows what it is, and mourns the singing it replaced.

## XIII. Of the Silence After

The war was over. This legend is the account of what it cost, and the account is short, for there was little left to put in it.

Of the elephantes, none returned; Hefferson was the most remembered, and in after years their very name began to wear down in the mouths of the young, and of all that they were, only their might was remembered; and the might had been so great that it overwhelmed the stories that carried it, and became, in the end, a thing to be afraid of in the night.

Of the Beevers, nothing.

Of the owls, a few; and a portrait of Robert upon a wall here and there — but the deed, time forgot.

Of the donkeys, fewer still. And of the conilios, the same.

Of the Councils, nothing was refounded, for the survivors were too few to counsel; and of the vigil fires, none were lit again, for the survivors had seen what a torch can be made to do.

Of the brandas and of those they hunted alike, kind after kind went out like lamps down a corridor, until the Wood that had held more kinds than any wood since the beginning held one of each, or none.

And of the Uursos, who had saved the Wood, only the clan of the Pooh remained, and they fewer in number than any. So the clan became the kind, and they were called simply the Poohs; and the word *Uurso* dwindled in the mouths of the Wood — *Woozlos*, I heard one call them — as *Hefferson* dwindled, wearing toward something else. So few were the Poohs that they did not dare the road north again; and Mr. Sanders took a house in the Wood among the survivors, beside the bee-tree — a house in a tree, that was handed down in his line through all the generations after.

And Sanders the Old, who had counselled a Queen in the morning of the world and buried a son at the evening of it, set a name over the door of that house — not his own title, and not in the old tongue, but the family name of the Girl, in her own letters, as she had taught him them long before: that there might stand in the Wood, for as long as the board should last, one written thing that was not a law, and not a decree, and not a warning, but a remembering.

The board says **MR. SANDERS**.

It was written with one big goal: that everyone should know where to find him — so that no long flight, and no fallen Robert, would ever be required again. But the legend does not end in the dark, and this is why. Mr. Sanders worked hard to restore the ways of the first age; he moved with haste for the first time in his life, and did not slow until it felt right; new songs flourished, new games were taught. He gave his last years to the erasing of councils and burrowcracies. Each season the animals cared more and more about hunny and adventures of wonder.

Of the war, none of the animals liked to speak, and so it was not spoken. And one spring, many years after, a cub was born to the line of Galleon — the last of the Poohs, born into a wood of orphans, heir to all their wisdom and knowing none of it. But the wisdom of the Poohs is a curious thing, and it is this: you do not need to know it to have it. A Pooh is not trying to be. A proper bear simply is.

*Here ends the second hand.*
