# THE LAY OF FANGORN

*[Here begins the second hand. It is not the hand of Sallie Sanders, whose writing goes before, nor is it like it. Of its author nothing is known: not the name, not the kind, not the year of the writing. The leaves are prose, and yet they were found titled a Lay; it may be that there was once a song, and that this is what remained when the song was gone. Once, and once only, these leaves call the war the Second; and they do not say why; and no other writing that survives says anything at all. The leaves were found bound together with the Books of Honey, and none can say who bound them.]*

---

# Chapter One
### *In Which the Wood Waits*

## I. Of the Departure

In the last year of the Blossoming the Girl came no more to the Wood.

She did not say farewell, for she did not know that she was going; that is the way of children, who leave a place a thousand times and then once. The animals waited at the edge of the trees through the turning of the leaves, and through the falling of the leaves, and through the snow. The conilios, who kept the calendar and the counting of days and such things, said she would come with the spring. The spring came, but Sallie Sanders did not. The flowers bloomed, and there was no sight of the girl who had loved to gather them, and give them to the animals.

While she walked among them there had been a measure in all things. The kinds were many beyond all later counting, and yet the Wood held them as a hand holds water, and did not spill. The wise said afterward that the measure was hers; that the Wood was full because her heart was full, and that so many kinds had never before dwelt together, nor ever would again. But this was said afterward. In those days none knew what kept the peace, and so none knew that it could be lost.

---

## II. Of the Vigil

Seasons came and went. By the second spring the conilios had grown so sure in their reckonings that they named the very day on which Geomy the gopher would put his head above the ground; and on that very day he did, and the Wood marvelled, and the conilios were proud, for it is no small thing to know the calendar so well that the earth itself keeps your appointments. But no reckoning named the day of the Girl; and when spring upon spring had come true in every particular but the one that mattered, it was plain to the conilios that the spring would not bring her of itself, and that the Wood must help her home.

So the idea was the conilios'; but at the assembly of the Wood it was the Leons who first offered to take the lead. And companies were formed of animals of every kind together, to walk the roads of the Wood and light them: fires at the edge of the trees, lit at dusk and fed till morning, so that if she were looking for the way back, the way back would be looking for her also. The conilios drew up the schedules and reckoned the turns; they ordered the wood-piles and the relief of the watches; they made of the waiting a working thing, as their kind makes a working thing of everything it grieves. Every company took its turn at the fires, but the ordering of the whole was conilio work, and was done well.

The companies of the torch-bearers were called the **brandas**, after the word for a torch in the old tongue; and the name was a gentle name then, and it is right that this be written down, because of what was done with the name afterward. And the brandas were mixed of every kind, and loyal within themselves, and each grew skilled in its own manner of hoping. The **Rupperts**, who were beevers for the most part, taught the others of their company the building of watch-towers. The **Abotts** — conilios and triggers mostly, who loved to leap and to hop as those kinds do — learned to write words of light upon the dark with their torches, that Sallie might read them from however far away she was; though the founders of the Abotts, the legend notes, were two pigs, and no one knows why, and it is usually the way of such things.

But hope, when it must wait, builds itself a house; and the house of hope is a committee. There were rosters to be kept, and turns to be argued, and disputes between the companies over whose fires burned at the best crossings; and so the companies sent elders to speak for them, and the elders began to meet whether there was business or not, and the meetings took a name — the **Councils**, as such things are always named. They sat in the great burrow of a conilio, it being the largest and the driest, and so anyone who took part in that ordering of things and of thoughts was called a **Burrowcrat**; and the manner of working that grew up among them, all rosters and rulings and turns to speak, came in time to be known as **Burrowcracy**, after the hole it was hatched in. Who knew how much paperwork and debate were needed to show the way home to one small girl. Thus the Wood came to be slowly governed, by the slow hardening of a vigil into an institution. The Burrowcrats and the Brandas were the children of the waiting. They never afterward remembered their mother.

The years passed. The fires burned. She did not come.

And the fear that the Girl had lost in the Wood, and forgotten — the fear the Wood had taught her to be free of, which had fled from her and found in all that time no place to rest — found a place at last in the growing worry of the animals. But it was a cunning thing, and would not be noticed; so it went first into only a few, and very deep, down where the gentlest feelings are kept, where no one thinks to look for it.

---

## III. Of Troki and the Trotskee

There was in those years a Leon called **Troki**, and the legend must speak carefully of him, for he was the best of his kind and the first of the dead.

Troki held that the Departure was not a fate but a problem; that what could be understood could be undone; that if the Wood could learn *why* she had gone, the Wood would know how to bring her back. He took this doctrine to the owls, who were the lettered folk and kept the records, and together they made a work of it: gatherings at the great oak where the owls had their library, readings of every record of her years among them, theories built and broken and built again. The branda took his name, as brandas did, and was called the **Trotskee**; and like all the brandas of those years it was mixed in kind — owls for the most part, for the work wanted reading, but with Troki the Leon at its head, and a few donkeys, and one elephante named Robert, whom they called **Bobby** to keep him distinct, Robert being a common name among the animals. The Trotskee was the highest of the brandas and the proudest, for it was the only company that could not only read, but stay awake while reading. It was the first of the torch-companies to carry its fire indoors, to the study of the question rather than the lighting of the way; and for a time it was the noblest thing in the Wood, and the oak on those evenings was the brightest tree in it.

But a search for a cause, if it goes long enough unanswered, becomes a search for a culprit. It happened here as it happens everywhere: by small steps, each one reasonable. If she left, something made her leave. If something made her leave, someone permitted it. If someone permitted it, someone is to blame; and blame, unlike the Girl, could always be found. The doctrine of the return grew a shadow, and the shadow grew faster than the doctrine.

And in those same years another Leon was listening, and saying little, and remembering one thing above all the records of the owls. For it was set down — a small thing, said once, the way children say things — that the Girl had told the animals of her own world, and had said that *in her world, the lions were the kings of the jungle.*

She had said it of another country, lightly, and forgotten it by supper. **Regulus** did not forget it. Of all the thousand thousand words of hers in the library of the oak, he carried away that one sentence; and in after years it was the founding text of everything he built, and he never quoted any other, and he never needed to. *She said it.* The only scripture of the regime that destroyed her wood was a true quotation, complete except for the world it belonged to.

---

## IV. Of the Burning of the Oak

On a night in the ninth year of the Departure, the great oak burned.

It burned with the library in it, and with the most part of the records of her years — not all, but most, and the most that mattered; and it burned with Troki in it, who was working late among the records, as he always was. By morning there was nothing of the brightest tree in the Wood but a standing black ruin, and the Wood gathered around it in a grief it had not felt since the Departure itself.

The Councils sealed the ruin of the oak that same morning, pending inquiry. The Burrowcrats took council over it as they took council over everything, in the great burrow, with rosters and rulings and turns to speak; and they reasoned — and the reasoning was sound, which is the bitterest part — that the guilty are drawn back to the place of their guilt, and that whoever came creeping to the ashes would name himself by coming. So a watch was set upon the ruin, and a board was posted upon it, in the fine legal letters of the owls' own school:

> **TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED**

And from that board the ruin took the name it bears in all these leaves: the Trespassers Oak.

The inquiry was never closed; in after years it was never meant to be, for a culprit found is a crisis ended, and no one has ever governed long without a crisis. The trap stood baited from that morning on, and caught nothing, ever; for the one it was built to catch had no need to creep back to the ashes — he passed them daily, at his ease, in the open, as a mourner. The only forbidden ground in all the Wood was the one place that held the ashes of whatever the truth had been; and the board that forbade it was written in the letters of the folk the truth would be blamed on.

On the next day the whole Wood took assembly, every kind of it, in a grief that wanted only a direction; and before the assembly came Regulus, and he came as that mourner, and he carried a testimony. He had sat in counsel with Troki, he said, on the very eve of the fire — the two Leons alone, as kin of one kind will sit — and Troki had told him a thing not yet told to any other: that the work was finished. That he had found it at last, the way to bring her home. Or so Regulus said. There had been no other ears at that counsel, and the one who could have gainsaid it was ash.

Then the Wood, which had lost a Leon, learned from Regulus that it had lost the Girl a second time; and grief of that size must be given someone to carry it. Regulus gave it the owls. Who else held the keys of the library? Who else knew what the books contained? And — he asked it gently, as one grieved to be asking — who stood to lose the most by her returning? For the Wood had already begun to forget what the legend here sets straight: that the Trotskee had been mixed of many kinds, that donkeys had sat in it, that Bobby the elephante had carried its lamps. The Wood remembered only that it was owls, mostly, who could read; owls, mostly, who kept the books; owls who were the highest branda and the proudest — and that the coming of Sallie would have ended their importance in an afternoon. The Wood heard this, and the Wood remembered something true, which is how the best lies are built: that everyone had always been bored when the owls spoke of the letters contained in their books; that everyone had nodded, and many had slept, through the readings; that the owls had treasured an importance no one else had ever quite seen the use of. Resentment lay ready, like dry wood. Regulus had only to set his testimony to it.

So the whole Wood came suspicious of the owls; and the legend, which is written in letters by a hand long after, will not pretend the suspicion was reasoned. It was only ready.

Whose paw held the brand that night, no leaf of these records tells, and no witness survived the telling. The legend does not accuse. The legend counts; and the count of that one night is this: by morning Regulus was rid of the only Leon the Wood loved better than it feared him; he stood armed with the whole Wood's grief; and before him sat the Councils, holding an emergency that no Council in the history of councils has ever had the courage to refuse. Whether fortune gave him these things, or he took them, these leaves do not say. It is to be marked only that fortune never gave him anything again; for after that night, he took everything himself.

And to the Councils, in the depth of the mourning, Regulus made his offer, and it was a masterwork, for it asked for everything and sounded like sacrifice. He had understood, he said, perhaps the half of what Troki had solved that last evening — and he said it in lettered language, with the smell of books upon it, though he understood little. But he understood enough to know that with what writings remained in the Wood, the work might be built again. Therefore let every book of the owls be gathered up and carried to the oak, where the work had lived; and let him alone be suffered to enter there, and continue what Troki had begun — for if the burning had been set to destroy the way back to her, then no owl, and no animal untrusted, must ever again come near what books were left. For this the Wood named him **Regent of the Trespassers Oak**: keeper of what remained of the way back to Sallie Sanders, *until she returns*. It was a small office, in words; it was one tree. But the key to that one tree was the key to the only door in the Wood that no one else might open; and much can be grown from one tree, by a patient gardener. And who was to doubt the regent of the return? *Until she returns* became *forever* on no day that anyone could afterward point to; and it was done, as it is always done, with the words of the thing it was killing.

# Chapter Two
### *In Which Robert Takes a Long Flight*

## V. Of the Suspicion

Then came the years of the question, and the question was: *who do you suspect?*

The Regent's testimony had planted the suspicion; the years grew it. The owls had been Troki's closest circle, the keepers of the burned records, the readers and the writers; and upon them the suspicion settled and stayed, fed from above and watered from below. Had they not been closest to him? Did they not keep their letters and their books — and what did honest folk of the Wood ever need reading for, anyway? What had been in those shelves, that someone wanted burned — unless the owls themselves had burned it, to hide what it told, and with it the one who had uncovered what they kept shielded behind their a's and b's and c's? The questions did not need answers. Questions of that kind are not asked to be answered; they are asked to be repeated.

At the first the Regent brought elders into the Trespassers Oak, to stand among the ashes and the gathered books, and hear what he was finding there. But in time he brought mostly the young, and the credulous; for he found them quicker to understand what he was understanding of the secret scheme of the owls. The elders asked questions. The young asked him to go on.

And the suspicion became the coin of loyalty. To doubt the owls was to love the Girl; to trust an owl was to spit on her memory; and any who said aloud that the questions did not hold together found the questions suddenly asked of *them*. Those who would not suspect were named, with a curl of the lip, **grege** — after the word for a herd in the old tongue: herd-beasts, flock-followers, the trusting and the led — for what else do you call the ones who believe the readers of books over the grief of the Wood? It was an insult, and it was meant to end the argument, and for a while it did.

But the legend sets down what the regime never understood: the owls kept records, and records do not grieve, and do not forget, and do not change their story in the retelling. And quietly, in burrow and thicket, one animal at a time, some of the Wood began to do the unforgivable thing. They began to believe the owls. And the insult, passing from mouth to mouth, fastened not on the cowardly but on the unconvinced, until *grege* meant simply: those who had looked at the regency and seen it. The animals of the after-years bore the name with honour, and it is the name this legend gives them; but let it be remembered that it was coined as a sneer, by the comfortable, against the clear-sighted.

For suspicion eats everything that can be suspected; and the old brandas, mixed of every kind, dissolved one by one, each kind drawing back among its own, until the animals of the Wood kept company only with their kin. The Wood had not always sorted itself by kind. It was taught to.

The brandas that remained, in those same years, ceased to carry torches for her returning. They carried them now in companies, by night, past the houses of the suspected, that the suspected might see the light under the door and understand. The work of hope had become the work of warning. The name was kept. The name made it worse.

---

## VI. Of the Dominion of the Fang

What followed has a long telling and a short one, and the legend gives the short one, for the steps are always the same and the reader of any age will know them.

The Regent's peace required guarding; the guard required strength; the strength gathered to it the strong. The Leons held the lawns under Regulus, who was not the largest of his kind nor the eldest, but the most cunning, and among Leons that is the only crown there is — and let none say the Rex could not fight, for the legend will come to the proof of it. The **Triggers** ran beneath his banner, striped and laughing, and their captain was **Ferox**, of whom it is written that he was never once afraid, and never once merciful. With them went the Weasels, who were small and went unseen, and the Jagulars, who waited in the trees and let go. These were the brandas now — and the leading of the brandas had been Leon work since the first fires — the torch-companies of the vigil, remade as the war-bands of the Fang, bearing the old name of hope as a weapon bears a flag.

Here the legend sets down what the Wood in all its after years never liked to say aloud: that in the Blossoming, and in all the time before it, no creature of the Wood had ever eaten any other. The fang was for show and the claw was for climbing; the measure had held in this also. What broke it was not hunger. It was an argument.

For the owls, who were slandered and shunned and had nothing left but their letters, stood up at last in the open assembly to defend themselves with words, which was the only weapon their kind had ever carried; and the words were good, and the Wood was listening, and the Leons were losing the argument. And tooth and fang ended what reason could not. The first flesh ever tasted in the Wood was tasted that day, in the open, in the middle of a debate; and the Wood looked away, and forgave it, and the legend records the two forgivenesses as they were spoken: *they were the traitors of the Wood* — and, said more quietly, and weighing more, *they were the only ones with feathers among them.* The first to be eaten are the ones who look least like the eaters.

But an argument is not a body, and cannot be eaten. The words of the owls had been sound, and sound words have this property, that they go on bouncing in a wood long after the voice is gone; and many an animal walking home from that assembly found the argument arriving again in its ear, and again at the supper, and again in the night, returning as echoes return, each time fainter and each time harder to answer. The Leons had won the day and lost something they could not name; for from that day there were those in the Wood who doubted what the Leons said — quietly, and to no one, but the doubt was seeded, and doubt of that kind does not need tending. It only needs time.

It was after that day that the board upon the Trespassers Oak was changed. Claws were drawn through the fine word of the law, and over it, scored deep in the crooked letters of the Triggers, who wrote by ear and by claw and spelled as the hungry spell, was nailed the new decree:

> **TRESPASSERS WILL BE EAT ON SITE**

Two hands on one board: the school-letters of the Councils beneath, the claw-letters of the Fang above. The whole history of the regency is written on that board — the law not repealed but written over, in worse spelling and to the same end; and the board changed when the first owl was eaten, and told the truth of the new order to any who could still read. What became of the board in the long years after is a small story, and a strange one, and it is not told here.

Then the hunting began. The owls were taken wherever paw could reach them — and it was the pawed kinds that did the taking, all of them and only them, for the new decree asked nothing of the hoof. So the owls were broken as a tribe: such of their books as had been hidden from the gathering were burned in the open when found, as the oak library had been burned in the dark; and thus the office created so that no book should ever again be lost came, in the end, to burn more books than the fire ever had. Their letters were made a mark of treason. Those that remained kept their learning the way embers are kept, banked and hidden and handed on; and the legend owes its own letters to that keeping, and says so here.

Then the donkeys did the bravest plain thing in all this history: they stood between. They had no letters and no theories and no part in any quarrel; they saw a kind being hunted, and they put themselves in the way of it, because it was wrong, and that was the whole of their reasoning, and it was enough. For this they were chased in their turn; and theirs was the worst portion of all, for the brandas hunted the owls for treason, as they said, but the donkeys they hunted for sport — not for treason, nor for reason, nor for hunger. The donkey's only weapon is the kick of his heels, and so the sport was this: to take the tail before the heels could answer. Many tails hung in the lawns of the Leons. In the **boggy land in the south-east** the donkeys were penned for it; and there, little by little, they were unstuffed — drawn out of themselves, handful by handful, until not enough remained within them, and they died hollow. And the bog where it was done is a gloomy place, and it is gloomy still, and shall be while the Wood remembers, though the Wood no longer remembers why.

---

## VII. Of the Rising of the Grege

At the hunting of the donkeys, the Wood that was left stood up; and thus the Second War began.

The most appalled of all were the elephantes, and the legend gives their reason as it was given, for it is not a reason that a maker of legends would dare to invent: *they share a similar tail.* The greatest kind in the Wood looked at the smallest of the persecutions — a tufted tail taken for sport — and saw its own. And **Herbert Hefferson**, eldest of the elephantes, called Old Hefferson, who had counselled patience through every slander and every burning, turned at last and began to walk toward the lawns of the Leons; and the herds saw which way the wisest and slowest to wrath was walking, and followed.

And to the elephantes came the conilios — the reckoners, the list-makers, the founders of the first brandas — and gave to the rising what they had once given to the vigil: rosters, and stores, and order. Among them was one braver than all his kindred, whose name was also Robert — for Robert, as has been said, is a common name among the animals — and to keep him distinct from the other Roberts of this legend, and from one above all, of whom it has much yet to tell, the grege called him **Rabbie**, in the manner of the north. It was Rabbie who drowned the warrens before the Weasels could come down them, and brought his kindred up out of the dark still fighting. The owls went also to the war, who had never borne arms and had the most to avenge; they fought as owls fight, by night and in silence, and the brandas learned to fear the quiet places under the moon.

Now the legend sets down a thing that the brandas never confessed and the grege never knew: the brandas were afraid. They had seen the size of the elephantes, and now they saw the order of the conilios joined to it, and in secret they were afraid; and fear in the cruel does not counsel retreat; it counsels more cruelty, for aggression is the compensation of the frightened. So the war grew worse than the persecution had been; and whatever had been pure in the brandas once — and something had been, for theirs were the paws that carried the first torches of the vigil — was lost in this way, and is always lost in this way: not at a stroke, but in the compensations of fear.

At the **Six Pine Trees** the brandas dug pits for the elephantes, and covered them with branches, and the great grey ones went down into the dark with their children. The pits remain.

Yet the grege lost, and lost, and lost. The legend of those years is a list of crossings given up and hedgerows burned, and the list is long, and it is not set down here, for the dead do not grow more honoured by the counting.

The grege were driven at last to the river, which was their wall and their trap. Beyond the river the **Beevers** had their lodges and their great dams, and the still water above the dams was the last safe country in all the Wood. There the remnant of every kind gathered, and the water stood between them and the Fang.

It is said that in that camp an old sow asked: *Is there no one in the world but us and them?*

And an owl answered: *There is the North.*

---

## VIII. Of the Flight of Robert

Of all that was done in the war, the legend holds one deed highest, and it was not done with tooth or heel.

For it was known, in the way that far things are known, that in the North, at the very top of the world, dwelt the **Uursos**: the honey-eaters, who take no flesh of any creature, and were therefore in no quarrel, and knew of none. Mighty beyond all the kinds of the Wood, and mild beyond all the kinds of the Wood, and far away. And of all the clans of the Uursos the quietest and the wisest was the clan of the **Pooh**.

**Robert the owl** flew in the black of winter, and he flew as the hunted fly, for his kind were proscribed and his errand was treason twice over: once against the Regent's law, which forbade the owls their wings beyond the Wood, and once against the Regent's deepest need, which was that the world outside should never hear. He flew over the lawns of the Leons by night, and over the leaping-trees of the Jagulars by day, and then over no trees at all, into the white, where an owl's wings are a poor argument against the wind. How many days he flew, none can say. The North does not count days in winter.

He came to the Pole at the end of his strength. The Uursos did not make him wait, for they do not make anyone wait; but there was no need of waiting and no need of words. **Sanders the Pooh**, Counsellor of the Uursos, looked up and saw an owl arriving at the top of the world in the dead of winter, and knew in that moment that something had gone wrong in the Hundred Acres, for no bird crosses the white for a small reason. An owl at the Pole in midwinter is itself the whole of the news.

What Robert said, or whether he had breath left to say anything at all, the legend does not know, and will not invent. He died among the Uursos before the morning, of the journey itself, which had been killing him for days and which he had outflown only so far.

They buried him at the top of the world, sitting upright, facing south, the way he had come.

---

## IX. Of the March from the Pole

The Uursos did not take counsel for long, for among them deciding and doing are neighbours.

They planted their standard at the Pole — a single pole of wood, bare, after the plainness of their people — to say to any who came after: *we dwelt here.* And then the Uursos went south, all of them, every one, and the North was empty behind them, and the snow came south that year as if it followed them.

At their head went **Galleon the Pooh, son of Sanders**, head of his clan and in those years the strong arm of all the Uursos: of whom it was said that the mildness of his people had been given to him entire, and also all the wrath they had never used. And beside him, for counsel, went Sanders the Old, who alone of all things living had seen the Wood that was before the Wood.

# Chapter Three
### *In Which The Poohs Stay*

## X. Of the Battle of the Ford

The brandas met them at the river, at the wide shallow crossing, for Regulus held that no army crosses a ford against a holding foe. In this he reasoned rightly about every kind but one.

The Uursos did not skirmish, and did not feint, and did not wait for morning. They came down to the water in the grey of the dawn and walked into it, and the river that had been the grege's wall became a road. What befell in the shallows the legend tells shortly: the Triggers broke first, who had never broken, and after them the rest.

It was the first victory of the war, and the grege upon the far bank saw it, and the sound they made was not like cheering. It was more like weeping, the wise say, for hope hurts worst when it arrives.

Of Sanders the Counsellor, the legend says that he marched, and that he did not fight. What he did in those years, these leaves do not tell, and it may be that this silence is his, and was the price of all that he told afterward. The legend keeps his silence with him.

---

## XI. Of the Trap at the Dams

Regulus had lost a battle, and the legend gives him this: he understood at once what manner of war had now come against him, and that it could not be won with teeth. So he set himself to win it with the weapon that had won him the Wood in the first place, which was deceit; and what he made of it the captains of after ages would study, if any age after had kept captains.

In the dark before a certain dawn the brandas fell upon the lodges of the Beevers and took the great dams, swiftly and at small cost, for the Beevers were builders and not soldiers. And then Regulus did the thing that none could read: he set his brandas to dig, and they dug trenches in the low meadow beneath the dams, and got down into them, and waited.

To every eye it was folly. An army in trenches in low ground, where the elephantes had only to come on and tread them into the mud — the brandas had given up the height, the open lawn, every advantage their kind had ever held. The grege looked on it and saw what they were meant to see: the Fang gone to ground, afraid since the Ford, holding a position that could not be held.

And the grege were proud, for they had tasted one victory after years of none, and pride is hungriest in those who have fasted. Proudest of all were the conilios, for they had made the calculations and drawn the plans, and their figures showed great advantage in the ground the brandas had chosen, the more so if the elephantes and the donkeys went at the front. The elephantes hung back, for they felt something amiss in it; and the conilios said that this was fear, and that figures do not feel. Then Old Hefferson spoke, and his words were kept: *"We will go first, and without fear, for we are ready to spend what must be spent to stop this madness, so that we may all go back to doing what animals do when they are not hunting one another. But something tells me there is fog in this clear plan of yours."* And they went first anyway — Old Hefferson and the elephantes at the front, the herds behind — down into the low meadow, against the trenches.

The vanguard of the brandas broke at the first shock and fled, swift and ragged, as routed soldiers flee. It was the finest piece of soldiery in all that war, for not one of the grege doubted it. They poured after, down into the trench-lines and the low ground, kind upon kind, until almost the whole strength of the grege stood crowded in the meadow beneath the dams.

Then the Weasels, who had been at the keys of the dams since the dark before dawn, let the still water remember that it was a flood.

Of the deaths of that morning the legend gives no number, for none was ever made. The water came down without malice and without mercy, as water does, into ground that had been dug deep on purpose to hold it. Old Hefferson was seen lifting the drowning onto his back until the water stood above his own, and was not seen after. The Beevers, who broke from their captors and dove into the breaking of their own work to hold it with their bodies, were not seen in the Wood again, then or ever. Their lodges stand empty above the meadows, and the meadows flood to this day in the heavy rains, for the dams were never built again; there was no one left who knew the building of them. It was the second time a reckoning of the conilios had come true in every particular but the one that mattered.

That ground is called the Floody Place by those who live there now, and they say it as children say it, lightly. Let them. The water has carried the rest away.

## XII. Of the Fall of Regulus

…and so by the high clearing at the top of the Wood the last strength of the Fang was gathered, and against it came up what remained of the grege, and at their head went Galleon the Pooh.

Regulus had measured him from afar, as the cunning do, and read him rightly: that the war now stood upon one Uurso, and that to bring him down was to bring down the heart of all the rest. So he forged the blade. He sent **Ferox**, and with him the whole Royal Guard of the Leons, picked fighters every one, with this charge only: *cut him from his folk, and finish him.*

They did cut him from his folk, on the slope below the clearing, a wall of the Guard between Galleon and all aid. And there the legend tells what the Guard was worth: it was worth some hours of the morning. Ferox came against him last, unafraid as ever, and unafraid he died. But every wound of that morning Galleon carried up the hill with him.

At the top, in the level green among the pines, Regulus was waiting; and let none say the Rex would not fight, for he fought then, and the legend gives him this also: it was the greatest duel that the Wood ever held or ever shall, the most cunning of the Leons whole and fresh against the mightiest of the Uursos bleeding from a morning of battle — and the Rex with all his craft and all his strength was not enough.

**Regulus fell, and the fang of him broke on the ground, and the regency that was to last until she returned was finished in the time it takes to say so.**

Galleon stood above him a little while. It is remembered that he asked for honey; and it was winter, and there was none. Then he went down upon the green beside his enemy, and did not rise. He had spent the whole of himself, and he had known the price when he carried it up the hill, and he had carried it up the hill.

When the Rex was seen to fall, the brandas did not surrender, for surrender must be offered to someone, and they offered nothing. They fled — east, and south, and out of all knowledge — and the Wood did not pursue.

The Uursos laid Galleon on the hill where he fell, among the pines. And this is set down as truth, witnessed by all who stood there: that bees came to that high place at his burial, in the dead of winter, when no bee flies; and none could say from whence they came; and they stayed upon him a while, and went.

Where he lies there grows the one patch of daisies in all that high clearing, the friendliest of flowers; and from that flower, the Poohs say, comes the best hunny in the Wood. The pines of that clearing stand round about in a ring, and they cannot be counted — some say three-and-sixty, some four-and-sixty, and no two countings agree — for they stand as mourners stand, and will not be numbered.

And the Wood, which had no monuments left to raise and no craft left to raise them, did the one thing that the poorest survivors can always still do: it made a song. It was sung first at the burial, and then at the fires, and then wherever two of the grege met; and it was called **Galleon's Lay**. The song is lost, as songs are lost — it was never written, for the writers were too few and the singers thought it would be sung forever. But the name of the song fastened to the ground where it was first sung, and the ground has kept it; only the mouths of the young, year wearing upon year, have rubbed the word smooth, the way a stair-stone is rubbed by feet, until *Lay* became *Lap*. The place is called Galleon's Lap to this day, by those who go up there when something solemn is to be done, and feel that the ground asks it of them, and cannot say why.

It may be that these leaves were given their title in memory of that lost song. The legend does not know. The legend is prose, and knows what it is, and mourns the singing it replaced.

## XIII. Of the Silence After

The war was over. This legend is the account of what it cost, and the account is short, for there was little left to put in it.

Of the elephantes, none returned; and in after years their very name began to wear down in the mouths of the young, and of all that they were, only their might was remembered; and the might had been so great that it overwhelmed the stories that carried it, and became, in the end, a thing to be afraid of in the night.

Of the Beevers, nothing.

Of the owls, a few; and a portrait of Robert upon a wall here and there — but the deed, time forgot.

Of the donkeys, fewer still. And of the conilios, the same.

Of the Councils, nothing was refounded, for the survivors were too few to counsel; and of the vigil fires, none were lit again, for the survivors had seen what a torch can be made to do.

Of the brandas and of those they hunted alike, kind after kind went out like lamps down a corridor, until the Wood that had held more kinds than any wood since the beginning held one of each, or none.

And of the Uursos, who had saved the Wood, only the clan of the Pooh remained, and they fewer in number than any. So the clan became the kind, and they were called simply the Poohs; and the word *Uurso* dwindled in the mouths of the Wood, as *Hefferson* dwindled, wearing toward something else. So few were the Poohs that they did not dare the road north again; and Sanders took a house in the Wood among the survivors, beside the bee-tree — a house in a tree, that was handed down in his line through all the generations after.

And Sanders the Old, who had counselled a Queen in the morning of the world and buried a son at the evening of it, set a name over the door of that house — not his own title, and not in the old tongue, but the family name of the Girl, in her own letters, as she had taught him them long before: that there might stand in the Wood, for as long as the board should last, one written thing that was not a law, and not a decree, and not a warning, but a remembering.

The board says **MR. SANDERS**.

But the legend does not end in the dark, and this is why. Mr. Sanders worked hard to restore the ways of the first age; new songs flourished, new games were taught. He gave his last years to the erasing of councils and burrowcracies. Each season the animals cared more and more about hunny and adventures of wonder.

Of the war, none of the animals liked to speak, and so it was not spoken. And one spring, many years after, a cub was born to the line of Galleon — the last of the Poohs, born into a wood of orphans, heir to all their wisdom and knowing none of it. But the wisdom of the Poohs is a curious thing, and it is this: you do not need to know it to have it. A Pooh is not trying to be. A proper bear simply is.

*Here ends the second hand.*
