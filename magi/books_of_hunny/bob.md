It was about this time, when Pooh was still small but not so small as he had been, that he met Bob; though "met" is not quite the word, and what really happened is a thing Pooh was sorry about for a long while.

He was walking — not going anywhere, just walking, which is the finest sort of walking there is — and thinking about hunny, as he generally was, when he felt something wanting to go *crunch* under his paw. Now a great many things go crunch in a wood, and most of them are leaves, and a leaf does not mind being stepped on, having no particular plans. But this nearly crunch was different. It sounded like *"Bob!"* — quite loudly.

Pooh lifted his paw, which is the first thing you do. And there, in the print of it, was a beetle: very small, very cross, and not nearly so flat as he might have been — for it is a lucky beetle that is trodden on by the fluffiest bear in the Wood, whose paws were more cushion than calamity, and who had only managed to *fold* him, which is undignified but mendable. Pooh did not know that yet. For one terrible moment he thought he had done the worst thing there is, which is to end somebody, by accident, while thinking about hunny.

"Pardono!" said Pooh. "I am so very sorry. I did not see you, because you are — " and here he stopped, because *small* seemed a rude thing to say to someone you had just sat on, even if it was the reason.

"Small," said the beetle, getting his legs back in their proper order one at a time. "Yes. I know. Everyone gets there eventually. You got there rather faster than most." And he said it without much heat, the way you say a thing that is simply true and cannot be helped, which made Pooh feel worse than any amount of shouting would have done.

Now Pooh did not know, then, the thing he came to know later: that being big is mostly a way of being able to hurt small things without meaning to, and that the whole job of being big, the only honest one, is to mind it. But he felt the beginning of it, that morning, standing over the folded beetle with his heart gone all wrong inside him; and feeling the beginning of a thing is how you start to learn it.

So he did the only thing he could think of, which was to make it right, as much as a thing like that can be made right. He sat with the beetle the rest of the morning. He helped him unfold, which mostly meant staying very still and being apologised *to*, since there was not a great deal a bear's enormous paws could do for so small a folding. And when the beetle was more or less himself again, Pooh said — I thought you *Bob*.

Oh, that's my name?

Oh ok, nice to meet you Bob.

And that's how he got to be named Bob, for no one has ever bothered to name him anything. Even though you got named for the noise you made by nearly being squashed by a fluffy bear, if you think about it, it isn't the most dignified way to come by a name — But the beetle tried it on, the way you try on a name, and found it fit; and *Bob* he was, from that morning, the first of all the Roberts there would ever be in that Wood, though neither of them knew it, and only one of them would ever find out.

The next thing Pooh learnt, he learnt from Bob, and it was the size of himself; which is a great deal to learn from somebody so small.

For Pooh was a lot of bear, and Bob was hardly any beetle, and that is the sort of difference you have to mind. A bear who does not mind it can fold a beetle without even meaning to — Pooh had very nearly done it once, and once was plenty. So now, wherever Bob went, Pooh carried himself the way you carry a full cup across a room: slowly, and all of a piece, and with the tip of his tongue out. He hadn't a word for it. He only found, in his paws, that there is a right amount of bear to give a thing — and that when the thing is a Bob, the right amount is hardly any bear at all.

Mu was pleased, in the quiet way that was the only way Mu had. She'd told him once that everything good comes in a right amount; and here it was again, wearing a beetle. "Gently does it," said Mu, who had fed the whole Wood and never spilt a drop — and Pooh, lifting Bob over a root on the tip of one claw, found that gently did do, and did it rather well.

Then there was the going slowly, which Pooh learnt because Bob went slowly, there being no other speed a beetle has. A whole morning's journey for Bob was about one step for Pooh, so that Pooh was forever arriving first and having to go back for him, and arriving first again, until he saw the sense in going at Bob's pace and calling that the right one. And the funny thing — Pooh told me this himself, long after, and still looked a little surprised — was that going slow was not the dull part before the good part. Going slow was the good part. A bear in a hurry misses very nearly all of the Wood; Bob, who could not have hurried to save his life, missed none of it; and Pooh, keeping Bob's time, found he was missing none of it either.

And the last of it Pooh learnt was that Bob ran out of Bob long before Pooh ran out of wanting to play. A beetle is given only so much beetle for one day, and spends it sooner than you'd think to look at him. Mica was the one who showed Pooh this, being the smallest of them all, and knowing better than any of the big ones what it is to be tired while everyone else is still going strong. She taught him to watch for the very moment Bob's legs began to argue with one another, and to stop the game a little before that and not a little after; because a game stopped too late leaves somebody crumpled in the grass, and a game stopped just in time leaves everybody wanting tomorrow. So Pooh learnt to carry Bob home on the flat of his paw at the end of a long day, very slowly, so as not to joggle him — and Bob would ride along half asleep, and that was a game too, and very probably the best one.

So my bear, who could have flattened the whole Wood and never felt the bump, learnt instead to go through it as if every last thing in it might be a Bob: slow, and careful, and watching for when the small ones tired. The strong are not gentle by accident. They are gentle on purpose, or they are not gentle at all; and that, said Mu, was about the size of it.
