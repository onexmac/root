# THE LEGEND OF FANGORN

*[Here begins the second hand. It is not the hand of Sallie Sanders, whose writing goes before, nor is it like it. Of its author nothing is known: not the name, not the kind, not the year of the writing. Once, and once only, these leaves call the war the Second; and they do not say why; and no other writing that survives says anything at all. The leaves were found bound together with the Books of Honey, and none can say who bound them.]*

-----

## I. Of the Departure

In the last year of the Long Peace the Girl came no more to the Wood.

She did not say farewell, for she did not know that she was going; that is the way of children, who leave a place a thousand times and then once. The animals waited at the edge of the trees through the turning of the leaves, and through the falling of the leaves, and through the snow. The owls said she would come with the spring. The spring came.

While she walked among them there had been a measure in all things. The herds were many and the hunters were many, and yet the Wood held them as a hand holds water, and did not spill. The wise said afterward that the measure was hers; that the Wood was full because her heart was full, and that so many kinds had never before dwelt together, nor ever would again. But this was said afterward. In those days none knew what kept the peace, and so none knew that it could be lost.

In the second year of the Departure the first blood was shed at the Six Pine Trees, and it was not shed for hunger.

-----

## II. Of the Dominion of the Fang

Then began the years that the grege — the herd-folk, in the old tongue — afterward called the Dominion, though the hunters called them nothing, for the strong do not name their seasons.

The Leons held the high ground and the open lawns, and over them was **Regulus**, who named himself Rex of all the Wood. He was not the largest of the Leons, nor the eldest; he was the most cunning, and among the Leons that is the only crown there is. He fought when fighting served, and schemed when scheming served better, and he was never once mistaken about which hour was which. The **Triggers** ran beneath his banner, striped and laughing, and their captain was **Ferox**, of whom it is written that he was never once afraid, and never once merciful. With them went the Weasels, who were small and went unseen, and the Jagulars, who waited in the trees and let go. Together these were the **brandas** of the Fang: the war-bands, sworn to the Rex.

Of the deeds of those years the legend is ashamed to be full. Let three things stand for all the rest.

At the **Six Pine Trees** the brandas dug pits for the elephantes, and covered them with branches, and the great grey ones who harm no living thing went down into the dark with their children. The pits remain.

In the **boggy land in the south-east** the donkeys were penned. The donkey is poor eating and the hunters knew it; they did not come to the bog for hunger. The donkey’s only weapon is the kick of his heels, and so the sport was this: to take the tail before the heels could answer. Many tails hung in the lawns of the Leons. The donkeys that were spared were spared as a cruelty, and the bog where it was done is a gloomy place, and it is gloomy still, and shall be while the Wood remembers, though the Wood no longer remembers why.

And upon the borders of the beech-lands the Triggers set up their boards, scored deep with claw-writing, of which one fragment endures, and the legend copies it as it stands:

> **TRESPASSERS WILL BE ATE ON SIGHT**

The hunters wrote with their claws and they wrote poorly, for their letters were a weapon and not an art.

-----

## III. Of the Rising of the Grege

In the fourth year the grege rose, for despair also is a kind of courage.

The first to lift his strength was **Herbert Hefferson**, eldest of the elephantes, called Old Hefferson, who had counselled patience through every burning and was not believed when at last he counselled war — until he turned and walked toward the Six Pine Trees, and the herds saw which way the wisest and slowest to wrath was walking, and followed.

The owls went also to the war, who were the lettered folk of the Wood and had never borne arms; they fought as owls fight, by night and in silence, and the brandas learned to fear the quiet places under the moon.

And among the conilios there was one braver than all his kindred, whose name was Robert — for Robert is a common name among the animals, and many bore it — and to keep him distinct from another Robert, of whom this legend has much to tell, the grege called him **Rabbie**, in the manner of the north. It was Rabbie who drowned the warrens before the Weasels could come down them, and brought his thousands up out of the dark still fighting.

Yet they lost, and lost, and lost. The legend of those years is a list of crossings given up and hedgerows burned, and the list is long, and it is not set down here, for the dead do not grow more honoured by the counting.

The grege were driven at last to the river, which was their wall and their trap. Beyond the river the **Beevers** had their lodges and their great dams, and the still water above the dams was the last safe country in all the Wood. There the remnant of every kind gathered, and the water stood between them and the Fang.

It is said that in that camp an old sow asked: *Is there no one in the world but us and them?*

And an owl answered: *There is the North.*

-----

## IV. Of the Flight of Robert

Of all that was done in the Second War, the legend holds one deed highest, and it was not done with tooth or heel.

For it was known, in the way that far things are known, that in the North, at the very top of the world, dwelt the **Uursos**: the honey-eaters, who take no flesh of any creature, and were therefore in no quarrel, and knew of none. Mighty beyond all the kinds of the Wood, and mild beyond all the kinds of the Wood, and far away. And of all the clans of the Uursos the quietest and the wisest was the clan of the **Pooh**.

**Robert the owl** flew in the black of winter. He flew over the lawns of the Leons by night, and over the leaping-trees of the Jagulars by day, and then over no trees at all, into the white, where an owl’s wings are a poor argument against the wind. How many days he flew, none can say. The North does not count days in winter.

He came to the Pole at the end of his strength. The Uursos did not make him wait, for they do not make anyone wait; but there was no need of waiting and no need of words. **Sander the Pooh**, Counsellor of his people, looked up and saw an owl arriving at the top of the world in the dead of winter, and knew in that moment that something had gone wrong in the Hundred Acres, for no bird crosses the white for a small reason. An owl at the Pole in midwinter is itself the whole of the news.

What Robert said, or whether he had breath left to say anything at all, the legend does not know, and will not invent. He died among the Uursos before the morning, of the journey itself, which had been killing him for days and which he had outflown only so far.

They buried him at the top of the world, sitting upright, facing south, the way he had come.

-----

## V. Of the March from the Pole

The Uursos did not take counsel for long, for among them deciding and doing are neighbours.

They planted their standard at the Pole — a single pole of wood, bare, after the plainness of their people — to say to any who came after: *we dwelt here.* And then the whole nation of them went south, every one, and the North was empty behind them, and the snow came south that year as if it followed them.

At their head went **Galleon the Pooh, son of Sander**, head of his clan and in those years the strong arm of all the Uursos: of whom it was said that the mildness of his people had been given to him entire, and also all the wrath they had never used. And beside him, for counsel, went Sander the Old, who alone of all things living had seen the Wood that was before the Wood.

-----

## VI. Of the Battle of the Ford

The brandas met them at the river, at the wide shallow crossing, for Regulus held that no army crosses a ford against a holding foe. In this he reasoned rightly about every people but one.

The Uursos did not skirmish, and did not feint, and did not wait for morning. They came down to the water in the grey of the dawn and walked into it, and the river that had been the grege’s wall became a road. What befell in the shallows the legend tells shortly: the Triggers broke first, who had never broken, and after them the rest.

It was the first victory of the war, and the grege upon the far bank saw it, and the sound they made was not like cheering. It was more like weeping, the wise say, for hope hurts worst when it arrives.

Of Sander the Counsellor, the legend says that he marched, and that he did not fight. What he did in those years, these leaves do not tell, and it may be that this silence is his, and was the price of all that he told afterward. The legend keeps his silence with him.

-----

## VII. Of the Trap at the Dams

Regulus had lost a battle, and the legend gives him this: he understood at once what manner of war had now come against him, and that it could not be won with teeth. So he set himself to win it with the one weapon the Uursos did not carry, which was deceit; and what he made of it the captains of after ages would study, if any age after had kept captains.

In the dark before a certain dawn the brandas fell upon the lodges of the Beevers and took the great dams, swiftly and at small cost, for the Beevers were builders and not soldiers. And then Regulus did the thing that none could read: he set his brandas to dig, and they dug trenches in the low meadow beneath the dams, and got down into them, and waited.

To every eye it was folly. An army in trenches under high water, in low ground, where the elephantes had only to come on and tread them into the mud — the brandas had given up the height, the open lawn, every advantage their kind had ever held. The grege looked on it and saw what they were meant to see: the Fang gone to ground, afraid since the Ford, holding a position that could not be held.

And the grege were proud, for they had tasted one victory after years of none, and pride is hungriest in those who have fasted. They believed the hour was theirs and the initiative was theirs, and they came on with all their strength — Old Hefferson and the elephantes at the front, the herds in their thousands behind — down into the low meadow, against the trenches.

The vanguard of the brandas broke at the first shock and fled, swift and ragged, as routed soldiers flee. It was the finest piece of soldiery in all that war, for not one of the grege doubted it. They poured after, down into the trench-lines and the low ground, kind upon kind, until almost the whole strength of the free peoples stood crowded in the meadow beneath the dams.

Then the Weasels, who had been at the keys of the dams since the dark before dawn, let the still water remember that it was a flood.

Of the deaths of that morning the legend gives no number, for none was ever made. The water came down without malice and without mercy, as water does, into ground that had been dug deep on purpose to hold it. Old Hefferson was seen lifting the drowning onto his back until the water stood above his own, and was not seen after. The Beevers, who broke from their captors and dove into the breaking of their own work to hold it with their bodies, were not seen in the Wood again, then or ever. Their lodges stand empty above the meadows, and the meadows flood to this day in the heavy rains, for the dams were never built again; there was no one left who knew the building of them.

That ground is called the Floody Place by those who live there now, and they say it as children say it, lightly. Let them. The water has carried the rest away.

## VIII. Of the Fall of Regulus

…and so by the high clearing at the top of the Wood the last strength of the Fang was gathered, and against it came up what remained of the free peoples, and at their head, as at every head since the Pole, went Galleon the Pooh.

Regulus had measured him from afar, as the cunning do, and read him rightly: that the war now stood upon one Uurso, and that armies would not bring him down, but a blade made for the purpose might. So he forged the blade. He sent **Ferox**, and with him the whole Royal Guard of the Leons, picked fighters every one, with this charge only: *cut him from his people, and finish it.*

They did cut him from his people, on the slope below the clearing, a wall of the Guard between Galleon and all aid. And there the legend tells what the Guard was worth: it was worth some hours of the morning. Ferox came against him last, unafraid as ever, and unafraid he died. But every wound of that morning Galleon carried up the hill with him.

At the top, in the level green among the pines, Regulus was waiting; and let none say the Rex would not fight, for he fought then, and the legend gives him this also: it was the greatest duel that the Wood ever held or ever shall, the most cunning of the Leons whole and fresh against the mightiest of the Uursos bleeding from a morning of battle — and the Rex with all his craft and all his strength was not enough.

**Regulus fell, and the fang of him broke on the ground, and the Dominion was finished in the time it takes to say so.**

Galleon stood above him a little while. It is remembered that he asked for honey; and it was winter, and there was none. Then he went down upon the green beside his enemy, and did not rise. He had spent the whole of himself, and he had known the price when he carried it up the hill, and he had carried it up the hill.

When the Rex was seen to fall, the brandas did not surrender, for surrender must be offered to someone, and they offered nothing. They fled — east, and south, and out of all knowledge — and the Wood did not pursue.

The Uursos laid Galleon in the lap of the hill where he fell, among the pines. And this is set down as truth, witnessed by all who stood there: that bees came to that high place at his burial, in the dead of winter, when no bee flies; and none could say from whence they came; and they stayed upon him a while, and went.

Where he lies, nothing has grown from that day to this. The pines of that clearing stand round about in a ring, and they cannot be counted — some say three-and-sixty, some four-and-sixty, and no two countings agree — for they stand as mourners stand, and will not be numbered.

The free peoples named the place **Galleon’s Lap**. The name endures, and shall.

-----

## IX. Of the Silence After

The war was won. This legend is the account of what the winning cost, and the account is short, for there was little left to put in it.

Of the elephantes, none returned; and in after years their very name began to wear down in the mouths of the young, toward a word for a fear in the night.

Of the Beevers, nothing.

Of the donkeys, one: grey, and young, and silent.

Of the owls, a remnant; and among them a custom arose of keeping a likeness of Robert upon the wall, that their children might know there had been one of their blood who flew the winter and was the turning of the war.

Of the brandas and their victims alike, kind after kind went out like lamps down a corridor, in the hungers and the sicknesses that follow wars as crows do, until the Wood that had held more kinds than any wood since the beginning held one of each, or none.

And the Uursos, who had saved the Wood — the Wood could not look at them. Their strength had been seen, and what has been seen cannot be unseen; and already in those first years the mild folk that remained were telling their children of great shapes in the winter mist, terrible in battle; and the tellings grew in the telling; and the old name of the saviours, written *Uurso* by the lettered and *Wurso* by the unlettered, had begun its long wearing-down into something that would one day frighten the very children of their children’s friends. The legend sets the true name here, plainly, that at least one page may hold it: they were the Uursos, and they came when they were called, and they asked for nothing after.

So the nation of the Uursos faded from the Wood as a nation, and did not return north — for the North was a country for a people, and they were a people no longer — and dwindled at last to one clan, and the clan to one house.

But the legend does not end in the dark, and this is why:

In the spring after the war a cub was born to the line of Galleon, son of a father he would never see — the last of the Poohs, born into a wood of orphans, heir to all of it and knowing none of it.

And Sander the Old, who had counselled a Queen in the morning of the world and buried a son at the evening of it, took a house in the Wood among the survivors, beside the bee-tree, and stayed. And over the door of that house he set a name — not his own title, and not in the old tongue, but the family name of the Girl, in her own letters, as she had taught him them long before: that there might stand in the Wood, for as long as the board should last, one written thing that was not a boast and not a warning, but a remembering.

The board says **MR. SANDERS**. It is the Wood’s first monument, and the only one that was ever painted with love.

The legend does not weep. The legend counts. And the count is this:

**Of the victory, little remained to be counted.**

*Here ends the second hand.*
